# M1-L5 – University: Linear algebra I – Vector spaces, bases and mappings {#L5}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor’s: mathematical foundations

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Core university subject · Linear algebra

## What is this about? {#S1}

At school, vectors were mostly arrows in two- or three-dimensional space. University-level linear algebra generalises this idea: lists of data, polynomials and functions can also form vectors if their elements can be added and scaled by numbers in an appropriate way. What matters are the **rules of calculation**, not the image of a geometric arrow. A basis describes the simplest building blocks from which every vector in a space can be constructed uniquely. A linear map transforms these building blocks according to rules that are compatible with addition and scaling.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

A file containing two measurements can be described as a pair. You can add two such pairs of data component by component and scale all the values by the same factor. With two suitably chosen basic directions, every pair can be represented. What works in the plane can also apply to long lists of parameters of an AI model and even to polynomials. Matrices record how a linear transformation changes the chosen basic directions.

Here, $n$ is a **positive integer** and gives the number of components. $W$ denotes a possible target vector space. The word **scalar** here means an ordinary real number that multiplies a vector. Other vector spaces can be defined over other number systems; for now, we stay with real scalars.

## What is a vector space? {#S4}

A real vector space consists of a set of objects and two operations: **vector addition** and **multiplication of a vector by a real number**. In particular, addition must be associative and commutative, there must be a zero vector and additive inverses, and the operations must satisfy the distributive laws. In addition, $1\cdot\vec v=\vec v$ and $(\lambda\mu)\cdot\vec v=\lambda\cdot(\mu\cdot\vec v)$ hold for real numbers $\lambda,\mu$. The symbols $1$ and $0$ here denote ordinary real scalars; $\mu$ (‘mu’) is a second scalar. Furthermore, addition and scaling must not produce elements outside the space (**closure**). These rules are chosen so that familiar algebraic manipulations can still be carried out.

The simplest example is the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, whose vectors are ordered pairs of real numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2,3)+(1,-4)&=(3,-1)\\
2(2,3)&=(4,6)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The vector two, three plus the vector one, minus four gives the vector three, minus one. Two times the vector two, three gives four, six.’ Addition and scaling are performed component by component.

Another surprising example is $P_2$: the set of all real polynomials of the form $a+bx+cx^2$. If you add two such polynomials or multiply one by a real number, the result is again a polynomial of degree at most two. A polynomial is therefore a **vector** here, even though it does not look like an ordinary arrow.

## Linear combinations, independence and bases {#S5}

A **linear combination** is formed when we multiply several vectors by individual factors and then add them together.

$$
\vec{w}=\alpha\vec{u}+\beta\vec{v}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Vector w equals alpha times vector u plus beta times vector v.’ The set of all vectors that can be obtained in this way is called the **space spanned** by $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, denoted by $\operatorname{span}(\vec u,\vec v)$.

Vectors are **linearly independent** if none of them can be expressed non-trivially as a combination of the others. More precisely, their linear combination can equal the zero vector only if all the factors are zero. A **basis** is a selection of vectors that is both linearly independent and spans the entire vector space. The representation of any vector in such a basis is unique.

**Mini-example – an unusual basis of the plane.** We choose $\vec u=(1,1)$ and $\vec v=(1,-1)$. Can we represent the vector $\vec w=(4,2)$? The conditions for the components are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha+\beta&=4\\
\alpha-\beta&=2
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the two equations gives $2\alpha=6$, so $\alpha=3$. This gives $\beta=1$. The result can be checked directly:

$$
3\cdot(1,1)+1\cdot(1,-1)=(3,3)+(1,-1)=(4,2)
$$

The two original vectors do indeed form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^2$, because neither is a multiple of the other. **Proof of uniqueness:** If $\alpha\cdot(1,1)+\beta\cdot(1,-1)=(0,0)$, both $\alpha+\beta=0$ and $\alpha-\beta=0$ hold. It follows that $\alpha=0$ and $\beta=0$, establishing linear independence. Conversely, for every pair $(p,q)$, we obtain the solution $\alpha=\frac{p+q}{2}$ and $\beta=\frac{p-q}{2}$; the whole plane is therefore spanned. The corresponding matrix with these vectors as its columns has determinant $-2$ and is invertible. The **dimension** of this space is two: every basis has exactly two vectors. In contrast, in the standard basis $\vec e_1=(1,0)$ and $\vec e_2=(0,1)$, the same vector would have coordinates $(4,2)$. **Coordinates depend on the basis; the abstract vector does not.** In our example, the coordinates $(3,1)$ relative to the new basis correspond to the standard coordinate pair $(4,2)$. Without specifying the basis, the same list of numbers can denote different vectors.

For $P_2$, for example, the set of three polynomials $1$, $x$ and $x^2$ is a basis. Its dimension is three, because each expression $a+bx+cx^2$ is uniquely described by exactly three coefficients.

## Linear maps: compatible with addition and scaling {#S6}

A map $T$ between real vector spaces is **linear** if it maps sums to sums and scaled vectors to correspondingly scaled results:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\vec{u}+\vec{v})&=T(\vec{u})+T(\vec{v})\\
T(\lambda\vec{u})&=\lambda T(\vec{u})
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘T of u plus v equals T of u plus T of v; T of lambda times u equals lambda times T of u.’ In particular, it follows that $T(\vec0)=\vec0$: because $T(\vec0)=T(0\cdot\vec v)=0\cdot T(\vec v)$, the image of the zero vector must be the zero vector. For finite-dimensional spaces, it is also sufficient to know the images of all the basis vectors: they determine the linear map uniquely.

**Mini-example.** The rule $T(x,y)=(2x+y,x-y)$ is linear. The input $(3,1)$ becomes $(7,2)$. The underlying matrix in the standard basis is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The **first column** shows the image of the first standard basis vector: $T(1,0)=(2,1)$. The **second column** shows $T(0,1)=(1,-1)$. The matrix can therefore transform any other input by combining its two columns:

$$
A\cdot(3,1)=3\cdot(2,1)+1\cdot(1,-1)=(7,2)
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Matrix A times vector three, one equals three times the first column plus one times the second column, giving seven, two.’ The table of numbers describes the linear map **relative to chosen bases**. If we change the basis, its matrix representation changes, not the underlying map.

## Kernel and image – what is lost, what can be reached? {#S7}

The **kernel** $\ker(T)$ contains precisely the inputs that $T$ maps to the zero vector. The **image** of the map contains all outputs that can be reached. If the kernel consists only of the zero vector, the map is injective: different inputs cannot produce the same output.

For the matrix just introduced, the determinant is $2\cdot(-1)-1\cdot1=-3$, and is therefore non-zero. Consequently, this map $T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ has a **linear inverse map**, and its kernel consists only of the zero vector. Its output can reach every vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Indeed, for a desired output $(p,q)$, the equations $p=2x+y$ and $q=x-y$ give $x=\frac{p+q}{3}$ and $y=\frac{p-2q}{3}$. For the output $(7,2)$, the inverse calculation gives $(3,1)$.

**Counterexample:** The map $U(x,y)=(x,x)$, which is also linear, has kernel $\{(0,y)\mid y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ and image only $\{(t,t)\mid t\in\mathbb{R}\}$. It is neither injective nor surjective onto $\mathbb{R}^2$. A kernel consisting only of the zero vector indicates **injectivity** for linear maps; for rectangular matrices, however, surjectivity need not follow. A non-zero determinant provides the corresponding criterion for invertibility only for square matrices between spaces of equal finite dimension. **M1-L6** develops these ideas further through rank, determinants and eigenvalues.

## What is it useful for? {#S8}

In computer graphics, rotations, reflections and scaling about the origin are linear maps; their matrices allow compact calculations that can be combined in sequence. An AI embedding is a list of coordinates in a high-dimensional vector space; matrices transform such representations into new feature spaces. In numerical equation solvers, large linear systems and the question of their solvability are analysed using rank and kernel.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S9}

- Not every transformation is linear. The translation $(x,y)\mapsto(x+1,y)$ is **affine**: it does not map the zero vector to the zero vector.
- The **choice of basis** affects the coordinate representation. The same geometric or abstract vector can therefore have different lists of numbers.
- Mathematical vector spaces do not have to be finite-dimensional. The examples discussed here are finite-dimensional.
- Real computers often store only approximations of the matrix entries. Numerical instability also depends on how strongly a transformation amplifies small errors. The mathematical existence of an inverse matrix does not guarantee that its numerical computation is well-conditioned.
