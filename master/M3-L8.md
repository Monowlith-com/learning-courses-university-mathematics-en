# M3-L8 – Master: Riemannian geometry – metrics, geodesics and curvature {#L8}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master’s-oriented advanced study following M1-L4, M2-L4 and M2-L6

## What is this about? {#S1}

From school geometry, we know the length of a line segment and the angles between arrows. In M2-L6, we considered which of these ideas survive on curved surfaces. **Riemannian differential geometry** makes this idea precise: at every point of a smooth space, we can compare small directions even if the space as a whole does not resemble a plane. This gives rise to **lengths, distances, angles, geodesics and curvature**. This is particularly important in robotics because the space of possible orientations is not an ordinary flat three-dimensional vector space.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a chequerboard pattern on a sphere. A small region can be drawn approximately flat when the points are very close together. However, as the location changes, so does the way the coordinate lines relate to one another. A Riemannian **metric** gives us a rule at each point for calculating the length of small displacements. By adding up these small lengths along a curve, we obtain its arc length. A **geodesic** moves without intrinsic sideways acceleration; locally, it is the natural substitute for a straight line.

A geodesic need **not** be the shortest path over an arbitrarily long segment. On a sphere, great circles are geodesics, but between opposite points there are several shortest great-circle arcs of equal length.

## Manifold and tangent space {#S4}

A **smooth manifold** looks like an open subset of a Euclidean space in a sufficiently small neighbourhood, even when its overall structure is different. The surface of a sphere is two-dimensional: locally, we need two coordinates. Its points, however, lie in the surrounding three-dimensional space.

The **tangent space** $T_{p}M$ contains the directions in which motion along the manifold is possible at p. On a sphere, this is the plane tangent at p. Tangent vectors at different points initially belong to **different vector spaces**. We therefore cannot subtract them from one another without an additional geometric choice. This is precisely the problem that motivates a **connection** and, later, the covariant derivative.

## The Riemannian metric as a local inner product {#S5}

A Riemannian metric assigns a positive-definite inner product on $T_{p}M$ to every point p. In local coordinates with tangent components u and v, we write:

$$
g_p(u,v)=\sum_i\sum_j g_{ij}(p)\,u^iv^j\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “We multiply each component of u by each component of v and the corresponding local metric entry, then sum all the contributions.” The matrix $g_{ij}(p)$ is symmetric and positive definite; for every non-zero tangent vector u, we have $g_{p}(u,u)>0$.

The length of an infinitesimal direction vector is obtained from $\sqrt{g_p(u,u)}$. Informally, the line element is written as:

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=\sum_i\sum_j g_{ij}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x^i\,\mathrm{d}x^j\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The square of a very small path length is determined by the coordinate changes and the local metric weights.” $dx^i$ denotes an infinitesimal coordinate element, **not** a new independent type of number. On the ordinary plane in Cartesian coordinates, the metric matrix is the identity matrix; the expression then simply reads $ds^{2}=dx^{2}+dy^{2}$.

## Complete example: the metric of a sphere {#S6}

Consider an idealised spherical surface with radius R. The polar angle $\theta$ is measured from the north pole (zero at the north pole, $\pi$ at the south pole). Longitude $\varphi$ runs around the axis of rotation. Away from the poles, we describe points by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=R\sin(\theta)\cos(\varphi)\\
y&=R\sin(\theta)\sin(\varphi)\\
z&=R\cos(\theta)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The horizontal distance from the polar axis is R times sine theta; we distribute this between the x- and y-axes using the cosine and sine of longitude. The height is R times cosine theta.” Taking the partial derivatives of these three components with respect to $\theta$ and $\varphi$ gives two tangent directions. Their squared lengths are $R^{2}$ and $R^2\sin^2\theta$ respectively, and their inner product is zero. The induced metric is therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
{(\mathrm{d}s)}^2&=R^2{(\mathrm{d}\theta)}^2+R^2\sin^2(\theta){(\mathrm{d}\varphi)}^2\text{.}\\
[g_{ij}]&=\begin{bmatrix}R^2&0\\0&R^2\sin^2(\theta)\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “A small change in polar angle corresponds everywhere to a length of R times the magnitude of the angle. A small change in longitude corresponds to R times the magnitude of the angle at the equator, but less near a pole.” Angles must be expressed in **radians**. The fact that the second diagonal entry of the matrix becomes zero at a pole indicates a **singularity of this coordinate chart**, not a geometric defect in the sphere. The local formula applies on the chart with $0<\theta <\pi$.

### Two paths of equal length {#abschnitt-7}

At the equator, $\theta=\frac\pi2$ is constant, so $d\theta =0$ and $\sin \theta =1$. An arc that changes longitude from zero to $\frac\pi2$ has length:

$$
L=\int_0^{\pi/2}R\,\mathrm{d}\varphi=\frac{R\pi}{2}\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “We add the local small lengths from zero to ninety degrees and obtain a quarter of the circumference of a great circle.” For an idealised sphere with $R=6\,371\,\mathrm{km}$, this is approximately $10\,007{,}5\,\mathrm{km}$.

A meridian arc from the north pole to the equator also has length $\frac{R\pi}{2}$. The fact that the angular coordinate $\varphi$ is not uniquely defined at the pole does not change the geometric arc length; for the calculation, we use another chart or a limiting process.

## Length, distance and geodesic – three different concepts {#S7}

For a piecewise smooth curve $\gamma :[a,b]\to M$, the metric defines its length:

$$
L(\gamma)=\int_a^b\sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma\prime(t),\gamma\prime(t))}\,\mathrm{d}t\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “At each instant, we measure the local velocity using the appropriate metric and add up its magnitude over time.” The distance between two points is the **infimum** of the lengths of all piecewise smooth curves connecting them, if such curves exist. In a general space, an infimum need not necessarily be attained by a curve.

An **affinely parametrised geodesic** is a curve whose covariant acceleration is zero. It is a stationary curve of the energy; when its speed is constant and non-zero, it is also locally stationary for length. On the sphere, great circles are geodesics; a circle of latitude other than the equator, by contrast, is not a geodesic. A great-circle arc of length at most $\pi R$ minimises length between its endpoints on the sphere; longer arcs are still geodesics, but no longer provide the shortest connecting path. For **antipodal** points, that is, points opposite each other on the sphere, there are even several minimising semicircles of equal length. The local geodesic equation alone therefore determines neither global minimality nor uniqueness.

## Why the ordinary second derivative is not sufficient {#S8}

A vector along a curve can change its **coordinate components** simply because the local basis directions move with it. Riemannian geometry uses the **Levi-Civita connection** for this purpose. It is uniquely determined by two properties: it respects the metric and has no torsion.

In local coordinates, the covariant derivative of a vector field $v(t)$ along a curve $x(t)$ is:

$$
\left(\frac{Dv}{\mathrm{d}t}\right)^k=\frac{\mathrm{d}v^k}{\mathrm{d}t}+\sum_i\sum_j\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x^i}{\mathrm{d}t}\,v^j\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The change in the kth vector component consists of the ordinary derivative plus correction terms accounting for the fact that the coordinate basis itself changes direction on the space.” The **Christoffel symbols** are coordinate coefficients, **not components of an ordinary tensor**. They can therefore vanish at a point in a suitable local coordinate system without the curvature vanishing.

Substituting the velocity vector $v=\gamma '$ gives the geodesic equation:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2x^k}{\mathrm{d}t^2}+\sum_i\sum_j\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x^i}{\mathrm{d}t}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x^j}{\mathrm{d}t}=0\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The ordinary second coordinate derivative and the geometric correction terms balance, so the intrinsic acceleration is zero.” This is an equation in local coordinates and applies to **affinely parametrised** geodesics.

## A concrete geodesic argument on the sphere {#S9}

For the sphere metric given above, the following Christoffel symbols are among those relevant on the regular chart:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}&=-\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)\\
\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi}&=\Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi\theta}=\cot(\theta)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

$\cot (\theta )$ is read as “cotangent theta” and means $\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$. This gives the two coordinate equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta''-\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta){(\varphi')}^2&=0,\\
\varphi''+2\cot(\theta)\,\theta'\varphi'&=0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The angular motions are coupled because a rotation in the longitude direction has a different geometric effect depending on the polar angle.” For an equatorial arc, we set $\theta=\frac\pi2$, giving $\theta '=\theta ''=0$, $\cos \theta =0$ and $\cot \theta =0$. Both equations reduce to $\varphi ''=0$. Thus motion along the equator parametrised uniformly by length is a geodesic.

For two non-antipodal points p and q on the sphere, represented as 3D vectors of length R, the **shortest** great-circle distance can also be calculated directly:

$$
d(p,q)=R\arccos\left(\frac{p\cdot q}{R^2}\right)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “We take the angle between the two radius vectors and multiply it by the radius of the sphere.” For $p=(R,0,0)$ and $q=(0,R,0)$, the inner product is zero, the included angle is $\frac\pi2$, and the distance is again $\frac{R\pi}{2}$. The real Earth is not a perfect sphere; geodetic surveying uses more accurate models. In numerical software, rounding errors may require the argument of the inverse cosine to be clipped to the interval from minus one to one.

### Countercheck: a geodesic is not necessarily a globally shortest path {#abschnitt-11}

We choose two points on the equator of the sphere:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p&=(R,0,0),\\
q&=\left(-\frac R2,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}R,0\right)\text{.}\\
\frac{p\cdot q}{R^2}&=-\frac12\text{.}\\
\text{Central angle }\alpha&=\arccos\!\left(-\frac12\right)=\frac{2\pi}{3}\text{.}\\
\text{Shortest great-circle arc: }\quad L_{\mathrm{short}}&=\frac{2\pi R}{3}\text{.}\\
\text{Other equatorial arc: }\quad L_{\mathrm{long}}&=\frac{4\pi R}{3}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The shorter arc spans one hundred and twenty degrees; the longer one spans two hundred and forty degrees.” **Both arcs lie on a geodesic**, but only the shorter is the shortest path between p and q. After a complete circuit of the same great circle, the curve is still a geodesic, but its endpoints coincide: the path length is $2\pi R$, while the distance is $0$. This is the concrete distinction between the *length of a chosen path* and the *distance between two locations*. In each case, we require $R>0$; we consider intrinsic paths on the sphere, not the shorter straight chord through its interior.

### Verifying the Christoffel symbols {#abschnitt-12}

Differentiating the sphere metric $g_{\theta\theta}=R^2$, $g_{\varphi\varphi}=R^2\sin^2\theta$, $g_{\theta\varphi}=0$ on the regular chart gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi}&=2R^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\text{.}\\
\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi}&=-\frac1{2R^2}\cdot\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi}\\
&=-\sin\theta\cos\theta\text{.}\\
\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi}&=\frac1{2R^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi}\\
&=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=\cot\theta\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “For the first component, we divide by the constant weight $R^2$; for the second, by the angle-dependent weight $R^2$ times sine squared theta.” The formulas require $0<\theta <\pi$. Longitude becomes undefined at a pole; there, a different local chart must be used instead of dividing by $\sin \theta =0$.

## Intrinsic curvature and a surprising comparison {#S10}

On a two-dimensional Riemannian manifold, **Gaussian curvature K** captures an intrinsic property. A spherical surface of radius R has everywhere:

$$
K=\frac1{R^2}\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The Gaussian curvature is the reciprocal of the squared radius of the sphere.” An ordinary Euclidean plane has $K=0$. Surprisingly, an ideal cylindrical surface also has $K=0$ **intrinsically**, even though it is visibly curved in three-dimensional space. A flat sheet can be rolled into a cylinder without stretching, but not into a sphere. This difference shows why **intrinsic** curvature does not simply mean “how curved does the surface look from the outside?”

In higher dimensions, instead of a single number there is the **Riemann curvature tensor** and quantities derived from it, such as **sectional curvature** and **Ricci curvature**. These concepts are related but not interchangeable. For the spherical surface, the scalar curvature in the usual two-dimensional convention is $\frac2{R^2}$, whereas the Gaussian curvature is $\frac1{R^2}$.

## What is it useful for? – applications in optimisation and robotics {#S11}

- **Orientation and navigation:** The space of all 3D rotations $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a smooth manifold; M3-L9 explains its additional group structure.
- **Manifold optimisation:** An update must remain within the admissible curved structure. An ordinary Euclidean step may leave it; retractions and exponential maps help with this.
- **Robot joints:** Recurring angles have periodic identifications. An angle coordinate can change at an artificial discontinuity even though the physical orientation remains continuous.
- **Geodesic navigation:** Distances on surfaces require their geometry; straight lines on a map are usually not the shortest paths on Earth.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S12}

The formulas require a smooth manifold and a **smooth, positive-definite** Riemannian metric. A *pseudo*-Riemannian metric, such as in relativity theory, is not positive definite and requires different statements. Coordinates may fail at poles or other chart boundaries even though the space remains smooth. Local statements about geodesics do not automatically guarantee globally shortest paths. A spherical Earth is a teaching model, not a precise basis for surveying.

## Connection to the next lesson {#S13}

M3-L9 explains how rotations form not only a curved manifold but also a **group**. Their infinitesimal directions of motion give rise to a **Lie algebra**. This allows rotations to be combined in a mathematically elegant way and parametrised reliably in robotics software.

## Key takeaways {#S14}

A **metric** determines local lengths and angles. The **Levi-Civita connection** enables geometrically correct comparisons of moving tangent directions. A **geodesic** has zero intrinsic acceleration and is only a shortest path *locally*. **Curvature** is an intrinsic property of a space, not merely its visible bending in the surrounding three-dimensional space.
