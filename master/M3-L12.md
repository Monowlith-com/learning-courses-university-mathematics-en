# M3-L12 – Master's: Nonlinear dynamics, optimal control and robotics {#L12}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master's-oriented advanced study following M1-L9 (differential equations), M2-L12 (kinematics and control loops), M3-L6 (optimisation), M3-L8 (geometry) and M3-L9 (Lie groups)

## What is this about? {#S1}

A machine does not simply move from one position to another. During the movement, velocities, joint angles, forces, distances to obstacles and the quality of the available measurements change. **Control theory** asks how to select suitable interventions on the basis of a mathematical model of motion. **Nonlinear dynamics** describes systems whose response cannot be captured by a single fixed matrix. **Optimal control** seeks control inputs that achieve an objective while keeping the required effort small. Real robots impose further requirements: motor limits, model discrepancies, delays and safe distances.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

A simple controller can pull a machine back towards its target position whenever it deviates from it. But pulling strongly in the opposite direction alone does not guarantee stability. For some systems, a local approximation is valid only very close to the desired state. A physically sound control design must therefore distinguish three things: **Is the target reachable? Does the state remain manageable under disturbances? Which control input is advantageous within the constraints?** Mathematics offers different tools for these questions: linearisation, Lyapunov functions, controllability, Riccati equations and model predictive optimisation.

## State models: why not all motion is linear {#S4}

We write a continuous dynamical system in general as:

$$
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),u(t))\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The rate at which the state changes is a function of the current state and the applied control input.” In a robot, x might contain both joint angles and joint velocities. The control input u could describe torques or motor commands. A specific model also requires **initial values**, for example $x(0)=x_{0}$.

For a linear time-invariant system, the right-hand side is particularly simple:

$$
\dot{x}=Ax+Bu\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “A matrix A describes how the state evolves on its own; matrix B specifies how interventions affect the state.” Real mechanics is often nonlinear, partly because of sine and cosine relationships between joint angles, friction and velocity-dependent couplings. Nevertheless, linearisation can be very helpful near an equilibrium.

## Equilibrium and local linearisation {#S5}

An equilibrium $x^*$ for a fixed control input $u^*$ satisfies:

$$
f(x^*,u^*)=0\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “At the desired state and with the appropriate control input, the instantaneous rate of change of the state is zero.” Near this point, we introduce small deviations $\delta x=x-x^*$ and $\delta u=u-u^*$. For a sufficiently smooth function f, the first-order Taylor approximation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta\dot x&\approx A\,\delta x+B\,\delta u,\\
A&=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{(x^*,u^*)},\\
B&=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right|_{(x^*,u^*)}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “A is the Jacobian matrix of the dynamics with respect to the state, and B is the Jacobian matrix with respect to the control input, both evaluated at the equilibrium.” The symbol $\approx$ is important: a linearised model is a **local approximation**, not identical to the original motion everywhere.

A particularly important limitation of linearisation is evident with just one state variable:

$$
\dot{x}=-x^3\text{.}
$$

The equilibrium is at $x^*=0$. The derivative of the right-hand side with respect to x is $-3x^{2}$ and hence is zero at zero. The linearised equation is $\delta \dot{x}=0$ and indicates neither decay nor growth. From this we must **not** conclude that the original system is unstable or stable. Here we need a different tool.

## Lyapunov functions – stability without an exact trajectory {#S6}

A **Lyapunov function** V is a kind of artificial energy that vanishes at the desired equilibrium and becomes positive elsewhere. If it decreases along all the motions under consideration, it can establish stability. For our scalar example, we choose:

$$
V(x)=\frac12x^2\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The artificial energy is half the square of the deviation from the target.” Its time derivative along the actual differential equation is given by the chain rule:

$$
\dot{V}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}x}\cdot\dot{x}=x\cdot(-x^3)=-x^4\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The change in energy is minus x to the fourth power and is therefore strictly negative for all non-zero x.” V is positive definite and grows without bound for large \|x\|; the dynamics is smooth and has a solution for all positive times. Thus $x=0$ is **globally asymptotically stable** for this system.

### Exact solution as an independent check {#abschnitt-7}

The differential equation can be solved for an initial value $x_{0}\ne 0$ by separation of variables:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-x^3,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^3}&=-\mathrm{d}t,\\
-\frac1{2{x(t)}^2}&=-t-\frac1{2x_0^2},\\
x(t)&=\frac{x_0}{\sqrt{1+2x_0^2t}},\quad t\ge0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The initial deviation is divided by a square root that increases with time and therefore tends to zero.” For $x_{0}=0$ the solution remains exactly zero. Unlike exponential decay, the return is slower here: for large times, the magnitude behaves like a constant divided by the square root of t.

**Counterexample:** For $\dot{x}=+x^{3}$ the same linearisation is zero, but $\dot{V}=+x^{4}$. A non-zero initial value moves away from the equilibrium; the system is unstable. **Two systems with the same zero linear approximation can therefore have completely different stability properties.**

## What does controllability mean? {#S7}

Stability asks whether a state remains close to the target or returns to it. **Controllability**, by contrast, asks whether suitable control inputs can make a desired state reachable in finite time. For a linear system $\dot{x}=Ax+Bu$ with n state components, the well-known rank criterion is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal C&=[B,AB,A^2B,\ldots,A^{n-1}B],\\
\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal C)&=n\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “We place the columns of B and their repeated images under A side by side; if this gives n independent directions, the linear system is controllable.” The criterion applies to **finite-dimensional, continuous-time, linear time-invariant systems without input constraints**. For nonlinear systems there are additional, more demanding criteria; the rank of a linearisation alone does not settle global nonlinear controllability.

### Fully worked example: the double integrator {#abschnitt-9}

We consider a one-dimensional position $p$ and velocity $v$. The control input u is an acceleration that can be chosen freely:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot p&=v,\\
\dot v&=u,\\
x&=\begin{bmatrix}p\\v\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},
\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\\
AB&=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathcal C&=[B,AB]=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The two columns are independent; the determinant of the controllability matrix is $-1$ and therefore non-zero. Thus $\operatorname{rank}(\mathcal{C})=2$. **Position and velocity are controllable in the idealised model by means of a suitable acceleration.** In a real robot, acceleration limits, obstacles and finite actuator capabilities substantially change the question of reachability.

## Optimal control: reaching the goal with reasonable effort {#S8}

In **optimal control**, we seek an admissible function $u(t)$ that minimises a cost function J. A common form is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(u)&=\int_0^T\bigl[{x(t)}^\top Qx(t)+{u(t)}^\top Ru(t)\bigr]\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+{x(T)}^\top Sx(T)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Over time, we sum the weighted state deviation and the control effort and, if appropriate, add a terminal cost.” Matrices Q and S should be symmetric positive semidefinite and R symmetric positive definite. Here the symbol `⊤` denotes transposition. The state trajectory x(t) depends on the chosen control input through the differential equation.

A large state term penalises deviations from the target; a large control term prevents excessively strong interventions. The weighting must suit the physical system: metres, metres per second or newton metres cannot be compared as equivalent costs without appropriate scaling.

## Fully worked LQR example {#S9}

The **linear-quadratic regulator**, abbreviated LQR, provides an analytically structured solution for linear models and quadratic costs under suitable conditions. We deliberately choose a scalar, unconstrained and time-invariant model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot x&=-x+u,\\
J(u)&=\int_0^\infty[{x(t)}^2+{u(t)}^2]\,\mathrm{d}t\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Without intervention, x already decreases; we may additionally intervene using u and want to minimise the sum of deviation and control energy over unlimited time.” We assume a quadratic optimal value function $V(x)=P x^{2}$, where $P>0$. The corresponding algebraic **Riccati relation** for $A=-1$, $B=1$, $Q=1$, $R=1$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q\\
&=-2P-P^2+1\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The two linear contributions yield minus two P, the control term minus P squared, and the state cost plus one.” The quadratic equation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^2+2P-1&=0,\\
P&=-1\pm\sqrt2\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “There are two mathematical roots, but only the positive one yields the required positive definite value function.” Thus:

$$
P=\sqrt2-1\approx0{,}41421356\text{.}
$$

The optimal linear state feedback is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u^*(t)&=-Kx(t),\quad K=R^{-1}B^\top P=P,\\
\dot x&=-x-Px=-\sqrt2\,x,\\
x(t)&=x_0e^{-\sqrt2\,t}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “We pull the deviation back proportionally with the factor square root of two minus one; the closed-loop system decays exponentially at the rate square root of two.”

### Checking the cost by direct integration {#abschnitt-12}

For $x_{0}=1$, we obtain $x(t)=e^{-\sqrt2 t}$ and $u(t)=-Pe^{-\sqrt2 t}$. Substituting these into the cost gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J(u^*)&=\int_0^\infty[e^{-2\sqrt2\,t}+P^2e^{-2\sqrt2\,t}]\,\mathrm{d}t\\
&=\frac{1+P^2}{2\sqrt2}\\
&=P\\
&=\sqrt2-1\approx0{,}41421356\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The integral of the exponentially decreasing state and control costs agrees exactly with the value P for an initial state of one.” The equality $\frac{1+P^2}{2\sqrt2}=P$ follows from the Riccati equation solved above. The control law is optimal **within this linear, unconstrained, infinite-horizon model**. With saturated motors, obstacles or an incorrect model, it is not generally the optimal or safe physical solution.

## How do we know that the LQR approach really minimises the cost? {#S10}

In this simple example, we can establish optimality without merely appealing to a named method. Set $V(x)=Px^{2}$ with $P=\sqrt2-1$. Along the motion $\dot{x}=-x+u$, we have:

$$
\dot{V}=2Px(-x+u)\text{.}
$$

Adding the instantaneous cost and V̇ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^2+u^2+\dot V
&=(1-2P)x^2+u^2+2Pxu\\
&={(u+Px)}^2+(1-2P-P^2)x^2\\
&={(u+Px)}^2\ge0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Because of the Riccati equation, the remaining coefficient of x squared vanishes; a non-negative square remains.” Integration from zero to T yields the exact identity:

$$
\int_0^T(x^2+u^2)\,\mathrm{d}t=V(x_0)-V(x(T))+\int_0^T{(u+Px)}^2\,\mathrm{d}t\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The finite-horizon cost consists of the initial value of the value function minus its terminal value, plus a non-negative integral of a square.” **Only if the terminal term $V(x(T))$ vanishes as T tends to infinity** does $J(u)\ge V(x_{0})$ follow. For $u=-Px$, the integral of the square vanishes and the closed-loop trajectory decays to zero. This control input therefore achieves the lower bound. For arbitrary control inputs without such a limit or without finite cost, admissibility must be established separately. This is not a general proof of optimality for all nonlinear systems.

### Admissible control inputs and the limit at the infinite horizon {#abschnitt-14}

The optimality proof from Section 7 can be completed more precisely for the specific example: with $\dot{x}=-x+u$ and **finite cost** $\int_0^\infty(x^2+u^2)\,dt<\infty$, in particular $u\in L^{2}(0,\infty )$ holds. The solution formula is:

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}x_0+\int_0^t e^{-(t-s)}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The initial state decays exponentially; the effect of all past control commands is added to it.” The convolution integral tends to zero for such square-integrable u: split it into an early part whose weight vanishes exponentially and a late part that becomes small because of the diminishing $L^2$ tail of u. Thus $x(t)\to 0$ and hence $V(x(T))\to 0$. For **every admissible control input with finite cost**, $J(u)\ge Px_{0}^{2}$ does indeed follow, with equality for $u^*=-Px$. This completes the optimality proof in precisely this class of models; for general systems, these limits cannot simply be assumed.

### Countercheck: an optimal LQR command can be physically inadmissible {#abschnitt-15}

Let us additionally impose a hard input limit $|u(t)|\le 0{,}2$. For $x_{0}=1$, the unconstrained LQR initially demands:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\sqrt2-1\approx0{,}41421356,\\
u^*(0)&=-P\cdot1\approx-0{,}41421356,\\
|u^*(0)|&\approx0{,}41421356>0{,}2\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The mathematically optimal initial command exceeds the newly specified admissible control input.” Because the unconstrained LQR solution is continuous, the limit remains violated even over a short time interval. The previous optimality proof **therefore does not apply as a solution to the constrained problem**. The optimisation must be reformulated with input limits, for example as a constrained optimal control problem or within MPC. Simply clipping the command may help in practice, but is not automatically optimal or guaranteed to be stable.

## Model predictive control: replanning after every measurement {#S11}

A real robot often has limits: joint angles must not become arbitrarily large, torques are limited and obstacles must be avoided. **Model predictive control (MPC)** calculates, at every decision instant, a sequence of control commands that is as advantageous as possible over a finite prediction horizon. It typically uses a discrete model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{k+1}&=F(x_k,u_k),\\
\text{minimise}\quad J&=\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\ell(x_{k+j},u_{k+j})+V_f(x_{k+H}),\\
\text{subject to}\quad&x_{k+j}\in X,\quad u_{k+j}\in U\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “From the current state, we simulate H future steps; we seek a sequence of interventions with the lowest possible total cost, which satisfies the admissible state and control input limits at every step.” Afterwards, **only the first calculated control command** is applied. Then the state is measured again and the optimisation is repeated. This repeated correction distinguishes *feedback* from a one-off, predefined open-loop sequence of movements.

**Why not simply use MPC all the time?** For nonlinear and nonconvex systems, the optimisation can have several local minima, exceed the available computation time or fail to find any admissible sequence under the chosen constraints. Mathematical guarantees of stability and recursive feasibility require additional conditions, for example suitable terminal costs, terminal sets and a sufficiently accurate model. A visually appealing simulated path is not a safety guarantee.

## Robotics: considering geometry and dynamics together {#S12}

In M2-L12, **joint coordinates** described robot geometry, and in M3-L9, elements of $\mathrm{SE}(3)$ described spatial positions and orientations. Dynamics adds velocities, inertia and forces to this geometry. In many manipulator models, the following symbolic relation holds:

$$
M(q)\ddot{q}+C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}+g(q)=\tau\text{.}
$$

Before reading the equation: $q$ is the vector of joint angles, $\dot{q}$ its velocity, $\ddot{q}$ its acceleration, $M(q)$ the inertia matrix, $C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q}$ the motion-dependent couplings, $g(q)$ the gravitational torques and $\tau$ the vector of applied joint torques. Read aloud: “Inertia times acceleration plus motion and gravitational contributions equals the applied joint torque.” This is an **idealised** manipulator model that does not include all friction, elasticity and drive effects. The specific matrices depend on the robot type, coordinates and model assumptions.

The connection to optimal control is therefore clear: joint angles must not merely change in a geometrically correct way. A physically possible sequence of torques must also exist that produces the path subject to dynamic and safety constraints. A real robot additionally requires verified state measurements, sensor error handling and independent protection mechanisms.

## What is it useful for? {#S13}

**Autonomous robotics** requires simultaneous control over position, velocity and admissible forces. **Drones and vehicles** combine local linearisation with more strongly nonlinear overall models. **Industrial automation** uses stability analysis and optimised trajectories for repeated movements. For **AI-controlled agents with physical actuators**, too, it helps to distinguish the prediction model, optimiser, controller and independent safety monitoring: a language-based proposal is not yet a physically verified control input.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S14}

- $\dot{V}<0$ outside the equilibrium establishes stability in this simple smooth scalar example. For general systems, the **domain, positive definiteness, invariance and existence of solutions** must be checked.
- Linearisation is **local**; a zero eigenvalue of the linearised system can leave the stability question unresolved.
- The Kalman rank criterion for controllability applies in the stated form to **linear time-invariant finite-dimensional** models without constraints. Nonlinear reachability requires other tools.
- The worked LQR is **linear, unconstrained and infinite-horizon**. Real actuator limits and obstacles are not included.
- For MPC, the existence of feasible sequences, real-time optimisation, model errors and recursive stability must be considered separately.
- Simulation results do not constitute safety clearance for machines. Physical devices require independently verified emergency shutdown, limits, collision protection and system validation.
- The methods presented here form a **Master's-oriented reading introduction**, not a complete lecture course in control theory with all proofs.
