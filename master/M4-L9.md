## What is this about? {#S1}

An AI model can often solve a task correctly and still be **poor at recognising when it is wrong**. It can give incorrect answers with great confidence, be fooled by small changes to its input or fail on unusual data. The mathematics of reliable AI therefore separates several questions: How often is a model correct? Are the probabilities it reports credible? Does its decision remain stable under bounded perturbations? And can it quantify the uncertainty of its predictions in a transparent way? No single metric answers all four questions.

## Basic idea: Accuracy is not calibration {#S2}

Suppose that for many individual cases a classifier claims to be correct with a probability of eighty per cent. If it answers correctly in only sixty per cent of a sufficiently large group of such cases, it is **overconfident**. This does not necessarily mean that every individual prediction is wrong; rather, the **reported probability and the observed long-term frequency** do not agree well. Another model may make fewer correct decisions but report its own uncertainty more realistically.

For a robot, this distinction is particularly important: a good average accuracy on familiar sensor data does not prove that the same algorithm remains safe in snow, under different lighting, with faulty sensors or in the presence of deliberate perturbations. The boundary between statistical reliability and **physical system safety** must remain clear.

## Probability calibration {#S4}

For case i, a binary model outputs the probability $p_{i}$ that the positive class applies. In the idealised sense, **perfect probabilistic calibration** means that among all cases for which the model reports exactly probability p, the actual proportion of positive cases is also p. In finite observed data this statement can only be tested approximately, often by grouping similar predicted probabilities.

A widely used coarse summary is the **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)**:

$$
\operatorname{ECE}=\sum_m\frac{\lvert B_m\rvert}{n}\cdot\lvert\operatorname{acc}(B_m)-\operatorname{conf}(B_m)\rvert
$$

In words: divide the predictions into groups. For each group, calculate the absolute difference between the observed accuracy and the average reported confidence. Weight this difference by the proportion of all predictions belonging to that group and add the results. **The ECE depends on the choice of groups** and is itself only an estimate. A small ECE therefore does not prove that all subgroups or each individual probability are calibrated.

### Fully worked example with two groups {#abschnitt-5}

We consider **ten binary predictions**, divided into two groups of five observations each. Here the reported values $p=0{,}2$ and $p=0{,}8$ represent the probability of the **positive class**. Group A: of five predictions with $p=0{,}2$, one is actually positive, giving $\frac15=0{,}2$. Group B: of five predictions with $p=0{,}8$, three are actually positive, giving $\frac35=0{,}6$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Group A: proportion }\tfrac5{10}\text{; observed positive rate }0{,}2\text{; prediction }0{,}2\\
&\quad\text{Contribution}=\frac5{10}\cdot|0{,}2-0{,}2|=0\\
&\text{Group B: proportion }\tfrac5{10}\text{; observed positive rate }0{,}6\text{; prediction }0{,}8\\
&\quad\text{Contribution}=\frac5{10}\cdot|0{,}6-0{,}8|=0{,}1\\
&\text{Total: }\mathrm{ECE}=0{,}1
\end{aligned}
$$

This example uses the *calibration error for positive-class probabilities*. The usual **confidence ECE**, in contrast, is based on the probability of the **predicted class** and its accuracy. For group A, the predicted class would be negative and the confidence would therefore be $0{,}8$; the negative class is correct in four out of five cases. This also gives $0{,}8$. Group B predicts the positive class with $0{,}8$ and is correct three times, giving $0{,}6$. The numerical ECE value in **this particular dataset** is also $0{,}1$. Terms and denominators must be clearly defined in real evaluations.

## The Brier score evaluates each individual prediction {#S5}

Another metric uses the squared difference between the predicted positive-class probability and the actual label:

$$
\operatorname{BS}=\frac1n\sum_i{(p_i-y_i)}^2
$$

In words: subtract the actual class label, zero or one, from each positive-class probability, square each error, add up all the errors and divide by the number of cases. Smaller Brier scores are better; under suitable assumptions the score rewards accurately reported probabilities and is a **proper scoring rule**.

For our example, we calculate each group:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{A: one positive observation, four negative; }p=0{,}2\\
&\quad\text{Sum of squared errors}=1\cdot{(0{,}2-1)}^2+4\cdot{(0{,}2-0)}^2\\
&\qquad=1\cdot0{,}64+4\cdot0{,}04=0{,}80\\
&\text{B: three positive, two negative; }p=0{,}8\\
&\quad\text{Sum of squared errors}=3\cdot{(0{,}8-1)}^2+2\cdot{(0{,}8-0)}^2\\
&\qquad=3\cdot0{,}04+2\cdot0{,}64=1{,}40\\
&\text{Total: }\mathrm{BS}=\frac{0{,}80+1{,}40}{10}=0{,}22
\end{aligned}
$$

This makes it clear why a model with an ECE of $0{,}1$ cannot simply be described as ‘90 per cent reliable’. The number $0{,}1$ is **not** the complement of a general probability of reliability.

### Counterexample: Zero ECE despite substantial subgroup errors {#abschnitt-7}

Suppose that a model outputs $p=0{,}5$ for the positive class **ten times**. In a subgroup A of five cases, all target values are $y=1$, and in a second subgroup B of five cases they are all $y=0$. Across all ten cases the positive rate is $\frac5{10}=0{,}5$: the error for a single combined ECE bin is therefore **zero**. Within A, however, the difference is $|1-0{,}5|=0{,}5$, and within B it is also $|0-0{,}5|=0{,}5$. The Brier score is $\frac{10\cdot{(0{,}5)}^2}{10}=0{,}25$.

The example does not prove that every prediction with $p=0{,}5$ is incorrect. It shows that **an ECE of zero with coarse binning can conceal substantial subgroup-specific miscalibration.** Subgroup evaluation, the number of observations and the conditions of deployment must therefore be taken into account when interpreting the results.

## Temperature scaling – how confidence scores are adjusted {#S6}

Many neural classifiers first output **logits**. For the class scores $z_{1},z_{2}$, softmax calculates class probabilities by exponentiation and normalisation. In *temperature scaling*, all logits are first divided by a **positive** parameter $T$:

$$
p_1(T)=\frac{\exp(z_1/T)}{\exp(z_1/T)+\exp(z_2/T)}
$$

In words: exponentiate the first logit after dividing it by the temperature, and divide by the sum of the exponentiated logits, scaled in the same way. A value of $T$ greater than one generally makes the distribution flatter, whereas a value of $T$ less than one makes it sharper. With a positive temperature, **the ordering of the logits does not change**, but the confidence may change.

**Numerical example:** Let us take $z_{1} = \ln  4$ and $z_{2} = 0$. For $T = 1$, the probability of the first class is $\frac4{4+1}=0{,}8$. For $T = 2$, it is $\frac{\sqrt4}{\sqrt4+1}=\frac2{2+1}\approx0{,}667$. The winning class is the same, but the reported confidence decreases. **Whether** this improves calibration must be determined on separate, representative calibration data. Changing the temperature alone proves nothing about data quality, subgroups or robustness.

## Robustness: What can small changes to the input do? {#S7}

An image may change only slightly while a classifier returns a different class. Robustness analysis asks which input perturbations **within the defined perturbation model** can change the decision. A simple example is a linear classifier with the decision score:

$$
s(x)=w\cdot x+b
$$

In words: the decision score consists of the dot product of the weight vector and the input, plus a threshold value. Values greater than zero are assigned to class A and smaller values to class B. For an additive perturbation $\delta$ with ${\Vert\delta\Vert}_\infty\le\eta$, the maximum possible decrease in the score can be bounded:

$$
s(x+\delta)\geq s(x)-\eta\cdot\lVert w\rVert_1
$$

Here $\eta$ (‘eta’) is a specified maximum change **per input component**, and $\Vert w\Vert _{1}$ is the sum of the absolute weights. The bound follows because, in the worst case, each component of the perturbation vector can change the dot product by at most the magnitude of its weight multiplied by eta. A **positive lower bound** proves that the class is preserved under this perturbation model.

### Fully worked numerical example of robustness {#abschnitt-10}

We choose $w=(2;-1)$, $x=(1;1)$ and $b=0$. The classification score is therefore $2\cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot 1 = 1$, which is positive. The one-norm of the weights is $|2| + |-1| = 3$. For $\eta  = 0{,}2$, the guaranteed lower bound is:

$$
s(x+\delta)\geq1-0{,}2\cdot3=0{,}4
$$

The decision therefore remains positive **for every** component-wise perturbation within $\pm 0{,}2$. For $\eta =0{,}4$, the bound falls to $1-1{,}2 = -0{,}2$: the previous guarantee can **no longer** be given. In fact, the permitted perturbation $\delta=(-0{,}4;+0{,}4)$ yields the score $s(x+\delta ) = 2\cdot 0{,}6 - 1\cdot 1{,}4 = -0{,}2$, resulting in a change of class. This proof applies to the specific **linear model and maximum norm**, not automatically to arbitrary neural networks or physical camera images.

## Aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty {#S8}

Two fundamental perspectives are distinguished:

- **Aleatoric uncertainty** arises from randomness or irreducible variability in the observation process. Further model training cannot always eliminate the nature of this variability.
- **Epistemic uncertainty** describes a lack of knowledge about the model or the data; under suitable assumptions, additional information can reduce it.

In practice, the decomposition is not always unambiguous. A Bayesian model, an ensemble or a sample of several predictions provides meaningful measures of uncertainty only under its assumptions. **Out-of-distribution (OOD)** means that the data encountered in deployment differ substantially from the training distribution. Good average results on test data from the original distribution do not automatically demonstrate good OOD performance.

## Conformal prediction: Intervals with a precise frequency statement {#S9}

**Conformal prediction** can provide prediction intervals or sets with finite-sample, distribution-free **marginal coverage guarantees** under the crucial assumption of **exchangeability** of the calibration cases and the future case. Here, ‘distribution-free’ does not mean that no assumptions at all are required.

We consider a fixed, trained regression model that produces a prediction $\hat{y}$ for an input. On a **separate calibration dataset**, the absolute residual error $r_{i} = |y_{i} - \hat{y}_{i}|$ is calculated for each case. Sort these errors in ascending order. For $n$ calibration cases and desired miscoverage $\alpha$, a common split-conformal construction selects the rank:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k&=\left\lceil(n+1)\cdot(1-\alpha)\right\rceil\\
q&=k\text{-th smallest calibration error (if }k\le n\text{)}\\
\text{Interval for new prediction: }\quad&
[\hat y_{\mathrm{neu}}-q;\hat y_{\mathrm{neu}}+q]
\end{aligned}
$$

In words: multiply the number of calibration cases plus one by the desired coverage proportion, round up to an integer position, and take the corresponding value of the sorted errors as the interval radius. If $k = n+1$, the threshold is formally infinite in this simple version; small calibration datasets do not permit every desired finite guarantee.

### Fully worked example {#abschnitt-13}

We have nine independent, suitably exchangeable calibration cases. Let the sorted absolute errors be:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&0{,}1;\ 0{,}2;\ 0{,}3;\ 0{,}4;\ 0{,}4;\ 0{,}5;\ 0{,}6;\ 0{,}7;\ 0{,}9
\end{aligned}
$$

For the target coverage of 80 per cent, we choose $\alpha  = 0{,}2$. Then $k=\lceil(9+1)\cdot(1-0{,}2)\rceil=\lceil8\rceil=8$; the eighth-smallest error is $q=0{,}7$. For the new model prediction $\hat{y}_{\mathrm{neu}}=10$, the interval is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[10-0{,}7\,;\,10+0{,}7]&=[9{,}3\,;\,10{,}7]
\end{aligned}
$$

**Why rank 8 corresponds to the 80 per cent guarantee here:** Under exchangeability and with ten **distinct** residuals (nine calibration errors and one new error), the new error has each of the ten possible ranks with probability $\frac1{10}$. The eight smallest ranks give $\frac8{10}=0{,}8$. When error values are tied, the usual non-randomised rule is conservative; the coverage remains at least as high. This calculation concerns the **marginal** probability over random calibration and test data, not a conditional guarantee for a dataset held fixed after the fact or for a particular subgroup.

The statement is that under the **exchangeability assumptions**, the actual target value of a new, exchangeable observation lies in an interval constructed in this way with at least the nominal marginal coverage of 80 per cent. Another requirement here is that the prediction model has been **fixed before evaluating these calibration cases**, so that the calibration errors and the new error remain exchangeable. Without separate data, for example in the case of uncontrolled overfitting to the same calibration points, the rank argument shown does not automatically apply. The interval does **not** promise to be correct for each individual object with an exact conditional probability of 80 per cent. Nor does it automatically remain valid if sensors, user populations or data collection change.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

- **AI classification:** evaluate confidence scores, calibration and the costs of errors together instead of reporting accuracy alone.
- **Medical or technical predictions:** make uncertainty transparent and, where appropriate, refrain from making an automated decision.
- **Robotics and image analysis:** analyse bounded perturbations of sensor data, recognise new deployment environments and examine the consequences of an incorrect signal in advance.
- **Language models:** distinguish model probabilities, the credibility of text, factual correctness and safety policies. A fluent response style is not a measure of uncertainty.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

1.  **Calibration is not the same as truth.** Even a calibrated model can make rare and severe errors.
2.  **ECE depends on the bins, the amount of data and the definition of the target.** In important applications, also report the Brier score, subgroups, distribution shifts and uncertainty intervals for estimates.
3.  Temperature scaling adjusts predicted probabilities but provides no general robustness or OOD guarantee.
4.  A robustness bound applies only to the specified input norm, model type and analysed set of attacks. Physical attacks may differ from these.
5.  **Conformal prediction requires exchangeability or explicitly justified variants of the theory.** With temporal drift or deliberate selection, the marginal guarantee may be lost.
6.  Neither good statistics nor a mathematical proof concerning an individual component can replace safe system integration, control of feedback loops or human responsibility.
