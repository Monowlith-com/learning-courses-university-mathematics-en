# M4-L6 – Compulsory elective: Reinforcement learning and decision processes {#L6}

**Course area:** M4 – Choice of study: AI, computer science and specialised fields

**Level:** University studies (elective / specialisation)

**Prior knowledge:** [M1-L7](M1-L7.md) (probability), [M3-L4](M3-L4.md) (Markov chains), [M3-L6](M3-L6.md) (optimisation), [M2-L11](M2-L11.md) (neural networks), [M3-L12](M3-L12.md) (control)

**Context:** Computer science / AI specialisation, from advanced Bachelor’s to Master’s level.

## What is this about? {#S1}

In ordinary supervised learning, a model receives inputs and corresponding target outputs. In **reinforcement learning** (RL), by contrast, a learning system makes **decisions about actions**. Its actions change the environment and influence which rewards and states can occur later. A locally good decision can be poor in the long term. Reinforcement learning therefore describes mathematically the problem of finding a strategy for many successive decisions through trial, knowledge of the model and evaluation.

## Basic idea: decisions have consequences {#S2}

A warehouse robot chooses between travelling quickly but riskily through a narrow passage and taking a slower, safer detour. A single immediate score is insufficient for evaluation: an accident may prevent future tasks, while a detour may consume battery power. RL considers **states**, **actions**, **transition probabilities** and **rewards** together. The central concept is the **policy** – a rule specifying which action is chosen in a situation.

A sequence of decisions in RL is not the same as a classical control loop from M3-L12: in control, a physical model may be given; RL can learn strategies from data. Hybrid methods combine both. For real robots, safety constraints must additionally be monitored.

## The Markov decision model (MDP) {#S4}

A **Markov decision process**, or MDP, extends the Markov chain from M3-L4 to include actions that can be chosen. Its classical description comprises a state set $S$, an action set $A$, a transition law $P$, a reward $R$ and a discount factor $\gamma$. In the Markov model, information about the current state together with the action should suffice to predict the distribution of the next state. There is thus no need to know all past states in addition.

The transition law in textual notation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(s'\mid s,a)&=\text{probability of the next state }s'\\
&\quad\text{after action }a\text{ in the current state }s\text{.}\\
&\text{For each admissible combination }(s,a)\text{:}\\
\sum_{\text{all }s'}P(s'\mid s,a)&=1\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “For a given state and a given action, the probabilities of all possible next states add up to one.” This statement applies to a correctly defined model. A physical robot may nevertheless encounter unfamiliar situations that are missing from the state space.

## Why long-term reward differs from immediate reward {#S5}

A decision-maker receives a reward at each step. Later rewards are often given less weight by a factor $\gamma$. For $0\le \gamma <1$ and bounded rewards, the infinite discounted expected value is finite:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_t&=r_t+\gamma r_{t+1}+\gamma^2r_{t+2}+\gamma^3r_{t+3}+\ldots\\
V_\pi(s)&=E[G_0\mid\text{initial state }s,\text{ actions according to policy }\pi]\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The total return begins with the immediate reward, followed by gamma times the reward at the next step, gamma squared times the subsequent reward, and so on.” With $\gamma =0$, only the first step counts. When $\gamma$ is close to one, later rewards receive greater weight. In this mathematical model, $\gamma$ is a valuation parameter – not automatically the actual probability that a system continues to operate.

**Counter-check at the boundary $\gamma =1$:** If a system receives $0{,}5$ points indefinitely by taking the action “always wait”, the undiscounted series $0{,}5+0{,}5+0{,}5+\ldots$ is unbounded. Equation $V=0{,}5+V$ has no finite solution. The general statements about finiteness and contraction above rely on $\gamma <1$ and bounded rewards. With finite episodes or other additional conditions, $\gamma =1$ can also be meaningfully defined, **but not under the same blanket justification**.

With a constant reward $r=0{,}5$ and $\gamma =0{,}8$, the geometric series yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G&=0{,}5+0{,}8\cdot0{,}5+{(0{,}8)}^2\cdot0{,}5+\ldots\\
&=\frac{0{,}5}{1-0{,}8}\\
&=\frac{0{,}5}{0{,}2}=2{,}5\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Someone who receives half a reward point per step indefinitely values this sequence at two point five in total when gamma equals zero point eight.” This is a mathematical discounted value, not a statement about actual service life.

## The Bellman equation: breaking a large future into many small steps {#S6}

The **Bellman equation** uses the structure “one step now, then the rest”. For a fixed policy and a finite state and action space, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_\pi(s)&=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\cdot\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\cdot\\
&\qquad\bigl[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V_\pi(s')\bigr]\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “For each possible first action, we weight all subsequent states by their probabilities. We add the mean immediate reward and the future value discounted by gamma; we then average over the decisions of the policy.” A possibly random immediate reward is captured here by the conditional expected value $R(s,a,s')$.

For the **optimal** discounted value of a finite, fully observable MDP, the Bellman optimality equation is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V^*(s)&=\max_a\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\cdot\\
&\qquad\bigl[R(s,a,s')+\gamma V^*(s')\bigr]\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “We choose the action for which the expected immediate reward plus the discounted optimal future value is greatest.” An optimal policy therefore maximises a **modelled expected value**, not automatically a guarantee of safety. For finite sets and $0\le \gamma <1$, the Bellman operator is a contraction with respect to the maximum norm; the equation consequently has a unique bounded solution.

## Complete worked example: wait or start an attempt? {#S7}

A simplified device is in an **active state S**. There are two actions:

- **Wait:** immediate reward $0{,}5$, then back to state S with probability one.
- **Attempt:** with probability $0{,}75$ the task succeeds and immediately yields $+8$; with probability $0{,}25$ it fails and yields $-4$. In both cases, the episode then ends in the **terminal state T**, whose future value is zero.

We choose $\gamma =0{,}8$. For the action “attempt”, the expected immediate reward is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[r\mid\text{Attempt}]
&=0{,}75\cdot8+0{,}25\cdot(-4)\\
&=6-1=5\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “In three out of four cases, one receives eight points; otherwise one loses four. The average is five.” For “wait”, we obtain the value $0{,}5+0{,}8\cdot V^*(S)$; for “attempt”, the value remains five. The optimal Bellman equation is therefore:

$$
V^*(S)=\max\left(0{,}5+0{,}8\cdot V^*(S),\,5\right)\text{.}
$$

Let us check the candidate $V^*(S)=5$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Wait}&=0{,}5+0{,}8\cdot5=4{,}5\text{.}\\
\text{Attempt}&=5\text{.}\\
\max(4{,}5;5)&=5\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, in this small model, **attempt** is optimal. Simply choosing “always wait” would yield only $2{,}5$ points according to the geometric series. “Wait once and then attempt” also yields $0{,}5+0{,}8\cdot 5=4{,}5$ points and is therefore worse than attempting immediately. These values are **expected values**: a particular attempt may still yield $-4$. Furthermore, the decision assumes that the specified transition probabilities and rewards are correct.

## Q-values and learning without a fully known model {#S8}

When transition probabilities are not known exactly, methods such as **tabular Q-learning** can update action values using observed transitions. A Q-value estimates how good a first action is when followed by a favourable strategy. The update uses a learning rate $\alpha$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{new}}(s,a)&=Q_{\mathrm{old}}(s,a)+\alpha\cdot\\
&\qquad\bigl[r+\gamma\max_bQ_{\mathrm{old}}(s',b)-Q_{\mathrm{old}}(s,a)\bigr]\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The new action value is the old one plus the learning rate times the correction error. The correction error is the observed reward plus the discounted best estimated value in the next state, minus the current estimate itself.” In the terminal state, the future component is zero.

**Worked example:** $Q_{\mathrm{old}}=3$, $\alpha =0{,}2$, $r=2$, $\gamma =0{,}9$ and $\max_b Q_{\mathrm{old}}(s',b)=4$:

**Important regarding the state boundary:** The following numerical value $4$ belongs to a **non-terminal** next state with admissible subsequent actions. If the episode ends with the transition, the term $\gamma\max_b Q(s',b)$ disappears entirely; a maximum must not be taken over non-existent terminal-state actions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Target value}&=2+0{,}9\cdot4=5{,}6\text{.}\\
\text{Difference}&=5{,}6-3=2{,}6\text{.}\\
Q_{\mathrm{new}}&=3+0{,}2\cdot2{,}6=3{,}52\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The current estimate of three becomes a new value of three point five two through a correction of zero point two times two point six.” This example is a **single tabular update** and does not yet guarantee that an optimal strategy has been learnt. Convergence statements for tabular Q-learning require, among other things, suitable step sizes, sufficiently frequent visits to state–action pairs and an appropriate stationary MDP description. These simple guarantees do not automatically apply to deep Q-learning with neural networks.

**Terminal counter-calculation:** Suppose the same old Q-value $3$ and the same step size $0{,}2$ apply, but the observed transition leads directly to a terminal state after a reward $-4$. Then the target value is simply $-4$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{new}}&=3+0{,}2\cdot[-4-3]\\
&=3-1{,}4\\
&=1{,}6\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The result differs from $3{,}52$ even though both calculations use the same update rule: in the terminal state, there is no further future return.

## Exploration versus exploitation {#S9}

An RL system must **exploit** known good decisions (*exploitation*) and **explore** potentially better, unfamiliar possibilities (*exploration*). In a simple $\varepsilon$-greedy strategy, the system chooses a currently best action with probability $1-\varepsilon$ and chooses randomly among all actions with probability $\varepsilon$. If two actions are possible and $\varepsilon =0{,}1$, then, for a uniquely best current action, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{best action})&=0{,}9+\frac{0{,}1}{2}=0{,}95\text{.}\\
P(\text{other action})&=\frac{0{,}1}{2}=0{,}05\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Ninety per cent of the time, the best known action is chosen deliberately. In the remaining ten per cent, the choice is random; half of these choices again select the best action.” In real systems, however, exploration is **not a licence for dangerous tests**. Safe exploration must take constraints into account or take place in a simulation beforehand.

## Bandits, partial observability, offline RL and language models {#S10}

A **multi-armed bandit** deals with decisions involving unknown rewards, but without the full state-dependent long-term dynamics of an MDP. A **partially observable** Markov decision process (*POMDP*) distinguishes the true state from an uncertain observation. The decision-maker then often needs a distribution over possible states, known as the *belief state*. This is particularly important for sensors in robotics.

**Offline reinforcement learning** uses existing collected data instead of freely trying new actions in the environment during learning. It can be safer and more cost-effective, but suffers from **distribution shift**: actions that rarely occur in the data are often difficult to evaluate. Not every offline RL strategy is therefore suitable for deployment.

In language models, RL is sometimes used to adapt the model towards preferred responses. However, the use of preference data, reward models or other optimisation methods is **not identical** to the small classical MDP example. In particular, a high expected reward in a training procedure is not proof of truth and does not guarantee safe responses.

## What is it useful for? {#S11}

Reinforcement learning is used for planning actions in games, simulated environments, technical resource management and certain robotics applications. The Bellman structure helps to weigh short-term costs against long-term returns. It connects **Markov models from M3-L4**, **optimisation from M3-L6**, **control from M3-L12** and **neural networks from M2-L11**.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S12}

In the example, the mathematical model assumes fully observed states, known transitions, a fixed reward model and finite state and action sets. In reality, sensors may be incomplete, data outdated and rewards poorly designed. **Reward hacking** occurs when a system increases the defined score without achieving the actual intended goal. Furthermore, extreme random events can be unacceptable despite a good expected value. Constraints, safety checks, evaluation outside the training data and, where appropriate, formal methods from M4-L4 are therefore important.
