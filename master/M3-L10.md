# M3-L10 – Master: Harmonic analysis, distributions and weak derivatives {#L10}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master’s-oriented advanced study following M2-L9 (Fourier analysis and the DFT), M2-L7 (Lebesgue integration and function spaces), M3-L1 (convergence theorems), M3-L2 (Hilbert spaces) and M3-L3 (weak PDE solutions)

## What is this about? {#S1}

An oscillation can consist of many frequencies. In M2-L9 we learnt how a Fourier transform makes these frequencies visible. **Harmonic analysis** studies the mathematical spaces and operators behind this process: When can a function be meaningfully transformed at all? How can energy be measured in the frequency domain? And what does a derivative mean when a signal has a jump and the ordinary derivative does not exist there? The theory of **distributions** answers the last question without defining away the jump as an ordinary number.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a sensor being switched on abruptly. The idealised signal jumps from zero to one. The jump has no finite classical slope, but it has a particular effect on every smooth measuring probe: the probe detects the entire change at exactly one location. Rather than treating the problematic ‘slope’ as an infinitely large number, we define its action on **test functions**. At the same time, the Fourier transform converts differentiation in the spatial or time domain into multiplication by frequency. Differential equations thus often become simpler algebraic relationships in the frequency domain.

## Which Fourier convention do we use? {#S4}

The Fourier transform is often defined using different normalisation factors. To avoid losing any hidden factors, we explicitly specify the **angular frequency convention**. First, for an absolutely integrable function $f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$:

$$
\hat{f}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\cdot e^{-i\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}x\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The Fourier transform of f at angular frequency xi is the integral over all positions x, where the function value is multiplied by the complex oscillation e raised to the power minus i times xi times x.’ Here $e$ denotes Euler’s number. An ordinary frequency $\nu$ in hertz corresponds to $\xi =2\pi \nu$ in this convention, provided that x represents time in seconds. Under suitable additional conditions, the function can be recovered by the **inverse Fourier transform**:

$$
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(\xi)\cdot e^{i\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘We combine all the frequency contributions again and divide by two pi because of our convention.’ For example, with sufficient integrability of f and f̂, the inverse integral representation is valid pointwise at suitable locations; for arbitrary $L^2$ functions, inversion must be understood **in the $L^2$ sense**. Other textbooks use symmetric factors $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ or frequency without $2\pi$. **Formulae from different conventions must not be mixed without adjustment.**

## A complete transform example {#S5}

Choose the symmetrically decaying function:

$$
f(x)=e^{-\lvert x\rvert}\text{.}
$$

It is integrable and square-integrable. We split its integral at zero. For $x<0$ we have $|x|=-x$, and for $x\ge 0$ we have $|x|=x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat f(\xi)&=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^x\cdot e^{-i\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\qquad+\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\cdot e^{-i\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\frac1{1-i\xi}+\frac1{1+i\xi}\\
&=\frac{(1+i\xi)+(1-i\xi)}{(1-i\xi)(1+i\xi)}\\
&=\frac2{1+\xi^2}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘To the left and right of zero, we integrate an exponentially decaying oscillation; after combining the two fractions, we are left with two divided by one plus xi squared.’ The transform is **real-valued, even and decreases as the magnitude of the frequency increases**. At $\xi =0$, we have $\hat{f}(0)=2$; this agrees with the integral of f over the entire real line.

The inverse transform is also plausible: in the frequency domain we have $\frac2{1+\xi^2}$, and in the spatial domain we have a signal with a sharp corner at zero. High frequencies decay only quadratically. Smoother, more regular signals typically have more rapidly decaying Fourier coefficients under suitable assumptions; a sharp transition leaves more high-frequency components.

## The Fourier transform as an energy decomposition {#S6}

The **$L^2$ norm** of a real or complex function is:

$$
\lVert f\rVert_2^2=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert f(x)\rvert^2\,\mathrm{d}x\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The square of the L-two norm is the area under the squared magnitude of the signal.’ On $L^2$, the Fourier transform is first defined for particularly well-behaved functions and then extended by continuity to all $L^2$ functions. The **Plancherel identity** in our convention is:

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert f(x)\rvert^2\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\lvert\hat{f}(\xi)\rvert^2\,\mathrm{d}\xi\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The signal energy in the spatial domain equals the frequency energy divided by two pi.’ Thus, with the transform convention chosen here, the relationship is not simply $\Vert f\Vert _{2}=\Vert \hat{f}\Vert _{2}$, but $\Vert\hat f\Vert_2=\sqrt{2\pi}\Vert f\Vert_2$.

**Checking the example:** For $f(x)=e^{-|x|}$, the spatial integral is:

$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\lvert x\rvert}\,\mathrm{d}x=2\cdot\int_0^{\infty}e^{-2x}\,\mathrm{d}x=2\cdot\frac12=1\text{.}
$$

In the frequency domain, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\frac4{{(1+\xi^2)}^2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi
&=\frac1{2\pi}\cdot4\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\\
&=1\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The familiar integral formula for one divided by the square of one plus xi squared gives pi over two; after normalisation the energy is also one.’ Both sides therefore agree. The formula provides a reliable bridge between filters, error measures and frequency analysis.

## Differentiation becomes multiplication {#S7}

For a sufficiently smooth function that decays quickly enough together with its derivative, integration by parts gives the following identity. One specific sufficient condition, for example, is that f is a **Schwartz function**. For general $L^{2}$ functions, this formula **must not** be read as an ordinary integral identity without suitable notions of differentiation. Within the framework of tempered distributions, however, the corresponding identity holds in the extended sense:

$$
\mathcal{F}(f\prime)(\xi)=i\xi\cdot\hat{f}(\xi)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The Fourier transform of the derivative is the transform of the function multiplied by the imaginary unit and the angular frequency.’ Differentiation therefore emphasises high frequencies. Correspondingly, for the second derivative we obtain:

$$
\mathcal{F}(f\prime\prime)(\xi)=-\xi^2\cdot\hat{f}(\xi)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Differentiating twice introduces minus xi squared.’ Under suitable conditions, this yields a method for solving linear differential equations: in the frequency domain, one often only needs to divide by a function of xi. **However:** dividing by zero or by a very small number may be impossible or numerically unstable, respectively. This leads directly to the inverse problems in M3-L11.

For convolutions in the appropriate $L^1$ setting, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(f*g)(x)&=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x-y)g(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\\
\mathcal F(f*g)(\xi)&=\hat f(\xi)\cdot\hat g(\xi)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Convolution adds shifted contributions; after the Fourier transform, this becomes simple multiplication.’ Linear time-invariant filters are based on this principle. The formula assumes that the integrals are meaningfully defined; more general function and distribution spaces require additional conditions.

## Distributions: measuring against a test function rather than at a point {#S8}

An ordinary function gives a numerical value for each x. A **distribution** T, by contrast, returns, for each suitable test function $\varphi$, a number $\langle T,\varphi \rangle$. The mapping is linear and continuous with respect to the test function topology. For a locally integrable ordinary function f, the mapping is:

$$
\langle T_f,\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘We let the test function phi weight the signal f over the entire axis and sum the contributions.’ The test functions in $\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})$ are infinitely differentiable and equal to zero outside a bounded interval. The pairing is therefore well-defined for locally integrable f. The function and the distribution it generates are mathematically **not the same object**, but they can be identified in a natural way.

A particularly important object is the **Dirac distribution** at zero:

$$
\langle\delta_0,\varphi\rangle=\varphi(0)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Delta zero returns exactly the value of the test function at position zero.’ It is **not an ordinary function that is infinitely high at zero**. The intuitive description of an extremely narrow impulse is an approximation by functions, not a pointwise definition.

## The weak derivative of a jump – a complete calculation {#S9}

Let the **Heaviside function** H satisfy $H(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $H(x)=1$ for $x>0$. Its value at exactly zero does not matter in this distributional calculation. Classically, H has no derivative at zero. We define the distributional derivative T′ by:

$$
\langle T\prime,\varphi\rangle=-\langle T,\varphi\prime\rangle\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The derivative of a distribution is defined by differentiating the test function and reversing the sign.’ For the jump, it follows that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle H',\varphi\rangle
&=-\int_0^\infty\varphi'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=-[\varphi(\infty)-\varphi(0)]\\
&=\varphi(0)\\
&=\langle\delta_0,\varphi\rangle\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Since the test function is zero far outside a finite region, only its value at zero remains.’ Thus, the following equality holds exactly **in the sense of distributions**:

$$
H\prime=\delta_0\text{.}
$$

This does **not** mean that the classical derivative of H exists at the jump. Rather, H has a generalised derivative operator whose action on test functions is exactly specified. This is useful for impulse excitations and discontinuous data in differential equations.

## Tempered distributions and the Fourier transform {#S10}

Not every distribution can be Fourier-transformed without further conditions. The usual class of distributions for Fourier analysis is $\mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R})$, the space of **tempered distributions**. They act continuously on the **Schwartz test functions** $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})$; these functions, together with all their derivatives, decay faster than any inverse power. The Fourier transform can be extended to these distributions by duality.

In our convention, for example, the following holds in the tempered sense:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F(\delta_0)&=1,\\
\mathcal F(1)&=2\pi\delta_0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘An ideal impulse has the constant value one in the frequency domain; a function that is constant for all time yields a delta distribution at frequency zero, weighted by two pi.’ The two relationships do not contradict the ordinary integral theorem: the integral of the constant one over all of ℝ is not absolutely convergent; it is precisely the **distributional extension** that is used here.

## Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces {#S11}

For engineering PDE problems, we often need not an arbitrary distribution, but a **weak derivative that is itself a square-integrable function**. The Sobolev space $H^{1}(\mathbb{R})$ consists of $L^2$ functions whose first weak derivative also belongs to $L^2$. A function is therefore not necessarily required to be differentiable in the ordinary sense at every point.

Our example $f(x)=e^{-|x|}$ has a corner at zero. Its weak derivative agrees almost everywhere with the function:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x)&=\begin{cases}e^x&\text{for }x<0,\\-e^{-x}&\text{for }x>0.\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both parts are square-integrable. **No delta impulse** arises, since f is continuous at zero and has no jump in its function value there. Hence f belongs to $H^{1}(\mathbb{R})$. The Heaviside function H, by contrast, does **not** belong to $H^{1}(\mathbb{R})$: on the positive half-line it is not in $L^2$, and its distributional derivative $\delta_0$ is not represented by an $L^2$ function. H is not in $H^1$ on any interval around zero either.

The distinction is central: **Distributional derivatives exist very generally; Sobolev derivatives additionally satisfy a particular integrability requirement.** A jump can thus be differentiated in the sense of distributions without already being an admissible weak solution of a particular PDE in the required Sobolev space.

### Cross-check: Second distributional derivative of a continuous function {#abschnitt-12}

The function $f(x)=e^{-|x|}$ is continuous, but its **first derivative** jumps at zero from $1$ (on the left) to $-1$ (on the right). An impulse therefore arises only upon taking the **second** distributional derivative. Away from zero, the ordinary second derivative equals $f(x)$; the jump of the first derivative is $-1-1=-2$. Consequently:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f''&=f-2\delta_0\quad\text{(in the sense of distributions).}\\
\langle f'',\varphi\rangle
&=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|x|}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x-2\varphi(0)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The second derivative contains the original smooth curve and an additional negatively weighted Dirac impulse at the corner.’ The formula can be checked independently in the frequency domain: with $\hat f(\xi)=\frac2{1+\xi^2}$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F(f'')(\xi)&=-\xi^2\hat f(\xi)\\
&=-\frac{2\xi^2}{1+\xi^2}\\
&=\frac2{1+\xi^2}-2\\
&=\mathcal F(f)(\xi)-2\mathcal F(\delta_0)(\xi)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both approaches yield the same distribution. This shows the difference between ‘the first weak derivative belongs to $L^2$’ and ‘the second derivative also belongs to $L^2$’: $f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})$, but $f\notin H^{2}(\mathbb{R})$, because $\delta _{0}$ is not an $L^2$ function. **The classification concerns equivalence classes of functions and distributional derivatives**, rather than individual arbitrarily defined point values.

## What is it useful for? {#S12}

In **signal processing**, Fourier operators explain why convolutions act as frequency filters and why differentiation can amplify high-frequency measurement noise. In **image reconstruction and AI**, smoothing priors and regularisers can be analysed in the frequency domain. In **PDE theory**, distributions and Sobolev spaces provide a precise language for weak solutions that have no classical second derivatives. In **robotics**, discontinuous control commands, idealised impulses and noisy sensor streams can be modelled separately using mathematics.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S13}

- Fourier normalisation is **not universal**; here we use angular frequency ξ and the factor $\frac1{2\pi}$ in the inverse transform.
- The ordinary integral definition requires sufficient integrability; the $L^2$ and distributional versions are **extensions**, not the same pointwise formula.
- $H'=\delta _{0}$ is an equality **in the sense of distributions**, not a classical equality of pointwise function values.
- Products of arbitrary distributions are **not generally defined**. Nonlinear equations in particular therefore require additional structure.
- Physical signals are limited in time and space. The idealised model of infinite domains, exact jumps or perfect impulses must be adapted to the actual measurement system.
- Weak PDE solutions are subject to space, boundary and regularity conditions that must not be equated with the mere existence of a distributional derivative.
