# M2-L10 – Information theory and coding {#L10}

**Course area:** M2 – Advanced Bachelor’s studies / transition to Master’s studies

**Level:** Bachelor’s / bridge to Master’s studies

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** University – further advanced studies

## What is this about? {#S1}

A text containing frequently occurring letters can often be stored more efficiently than an entirely random sequence of characters. Why is that? Information theory describes how surprising individual events are and how much uncertainty a message source produces on average. From this, we can derive limits on lossless compression. A second area concerns transmission: data may be corrupted, so we need codes that detect or even correct errors. This is not a philosophical enquiry into the linguistic ‘meaning’ of a message, but a precise mathematical examination of its statistical structure.

The lesson builds on probability from M1-L7, logarithms from School Mathematics M1-L19 and discrete structures from M2-L1.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Suppose a device almost always reports ‘Everything is fine’ and only rarely reports ‘Error’. The usual message surprises us very little; the rare message conveys more new information. If we use short bit sequences for frequent messages and longer ones for rare messages, we can save storage space. Conversely, when transmitting data, we sometimes deliberately send **additional bits** so that corruption can be detected. Compression removes statistical redundancy; error correction introduces redundancy in a controlled manner. These goals do not contradict each other because they apply at different stages of processing.

## The surprise of an event {#S4}

The information content of an observed message is defined as:

$$
I(x)=-\log_2(p(x))
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The information content of x is minus the logarithm to base two of its probability, p of x.’ An event that occurs with certainty, with $p(x)=1$, yields $I(x)=0$ bits, since observing it eliminates no uncertainty. For $p(x)=\frac{1}{8}$, the information content is $-\log_2\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)=3$ bits.

The formula assumes $p(x)>0$. An impossible event is not observed under the assumed model; its formal self-information would not be finite. A high information value does **not** prove that a message is socially, scientifically or personally important.

## Shannon entropy: information on average {#S5}

A message source produces different symbols with different probabilities. Its Shannon entropy is the **expected** information content:

$$
H(X)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2(p_i)
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The entropy H of X is minus the sum over all possible symbol indices i, of the probability p sub i multiplied by the logarithm to base two of that probability.’ Each type of symbol contributes its information content weighted by its frequency. For $p_{i}=0$, we use the limit and set the corresponding contribution to the sum to zero.

A single uniformly distributed bit (`0` and $1$ each with probability $\frac{1}{2}$) has exactly one bit of entropy. When the outcomes have very unequal probabilities, the average is smaller. Entropy is expressed in **bits per symbol** when calculations use $\log_2$.

## Fully explained mini-example: four messages {#S6}

A system sends messages A, B, C and D with probabilities $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{8}$ and $\frac{1}{8}$.

| Message | Probability | Self-information | One possible prefix code | Code length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | $\frac12$ | 1 bit | `0` | 1 bit |
| B | $\frac14$ | 2 bits | `10` | 2 bits |
| C | $\frac18$ | 3 bits | `110` | 3 bits |
| D | $\frac18$ | 3 bits | `111` | 3 bits |

The mean information content is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(X)&=\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot2+\frac18\cdot3+\frac18\cdot3\\
&=0{,}5+0{,}5+0{,}375+0{,}375\\
&=1{,}75\,\text{bits per symbol}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Spoken:** ‘For each message, we multiply its probability by its information content and add the four contributions. On average, each message has an information content of one point seven five bits.’

The chosen code is **prefix-free**: no complete codeword is the beginning of another. Therefore, the receiver can divide the sequence of bits unambiguously without an additional separator. The code also requires an average of $1{,}75$ bits per message: $\frac{1}{2}\cdot1+\frac{1}{4}\cdot2+\frac{1}{8}\cdot3+\frac{1}{8}\cdot3$. A simple fixed-length code for four symbols would always require two bits. In this idealised model with a known distribution, we save an average of $0{,}25$ bits per symbol.

**Important limitation:** For a known discrete source and uniquely decodable binary codes, the average lossless code length in the usual source coding model is no less than the entropy. However, a particular compression method does not always achieve the entropy exactly; block coding, code management and metadata may require additional bits. The perfect agreement above is a favourable example.

## Huffman coding and lossy compression {#S7}

Huffman codes gradually combine rare symbols into a code tree and produce **prefix-free variable-length codewords**. Given known symbol probabilities, they provide an optimal mean length among binary prefix codes that encode individual symbols. The symbol probabilities must be appropriate for the data; an unsuitable model may reduce compression efficiency.

Lossless compression allows **bit-for-bit reconstruction** of the original data. In lossy compression, by contrast, certain differences are accepted: audio signals, images or videos may be reconstructed approximately. This requires a quality measure (such as perceptible deviation) – simple Shannon entropy alone does not determine meaningful image or audio quality. The mathematical trade-off between data rate and permitted distortion is called **rate–distortion theory**.

## Error detection and error correction {#S8}

Transmission may alter bits. A simple **parity bit** extends a bit sequence so that, for example, it contains an even number of ones.

**Mini-example of error detection:** The data sequence `1011` contains three ones. We append $1$, so that the transmitted sequence `10111` contains four ones in total. If a single bit is flipped, the receiver obtains an odd number of ones and detects an error. **However, this does not tell the receiver which bit is incorrect.** Two bit errors may remain undetected.

For actual error correction, a code needs larger distances between permitted codewords. The **Hamming distance** counts the bit positions at which two words of equal length differ. If the minimum distance between distinct valid codewords is $d_{\min}$, a suitable decoder is guaranteed to **detect** up to $d_{\min}-1$ bit errors and **uniquely correct** up to $\left\lfloor\frac{d_{\min}-1}{2}\right\rfloor$ errors. The downward-facing bracket notation `⌊...⌋` means rounding down to the next lower integer.

A classical Hamming code $(7,4)$ carries four data bits in seven transmitted bits, and its valid codewords have a minimum Hamming distance of **$d_{\min}=3$**. Therefore, a single bit error can be corrected unambiguously; with two bit errors, reliable correction is **not** guaranteed. If single-error correction is applied blindly to two errors, an incorrect codeword may be returned. Only a pure codeword validity check (without attempting error correction) reliably detects up to two bit errors. The exact parity-check equations and the structure of the code are possible topics for further study in their own right.

## Cross-entropy as a bridge to AI {#S9}

M1-L7 and M2-L11 deal with prediction probabilities. If the true relative probabilities of a source are $p_{i}$, but a model predicts $q_{i}$, then the **cross-entropy** measures the mean negative logarithmic score of this prediction:

$$
H(p,q)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2(q_i)
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The cross-entropy of p and q is minus the sum of the true probabilities p sub i multiplied by the logarithm to base two of the predicted probabilities q sub i.’ For positive $p_{i}$, the corresponding $q_{i}$ must be positive for the value to remain finite. Cross-entropy is **not the same** as the entropy of the data source: it additionally includes the cost of an unsuitable prediction model. Many AI libraries use the natural logarithm $\ln$; the unit is then the *nat* rather than the bit. The distributions $p$ and $q$ must each be **normalised to one**; only where $p_{i}>0$ must $q_{i}>0$ hold if cross-entropy is to be finite.

**Numerical check for an unsuitable model:** Let $p=\left(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{4}\right)$ be the true distribution and $q=\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)$ the model prediction. Using logarithms to base two gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(p,q)&=-\frac34\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac12\right)-\frac14\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac12\right)=1\,\text{bit}\\
H(p)&=-\frac34\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac34\right)-\frac14\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac14\right)\\
&\approx 0{,}8113\,\text{bits}\\
H(p,q)-H(p)&\approx0{,}1887\,\text{bits}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Spoken:** The model receives an average score of one bit, although the true symbol distribution has an entropy of only approximately zero point eight one one three bits. The additional amount is the Kullback–Leibler divergence in this direction, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$. It is never negative for valid discrete distributions. The value describes how well the model fits, not the meaning of the two messages.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

Information theory explains the foundations of lossless file compression, communication protocols and data transmission. Error-correcting codes are used in storage media and radio links, among other applications. Entropy and cross-entropy are used in data analysis, language models and probabilistic classifiers. When designing a software architecture of your own, the distinction between **compression, transmission, checking and decoding** is particularly helpful.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

The entropy formula describes a **known probability distribution**, not the objective meaning of a message. Real data contain dependencies spanning many symbols. The **entropy of an individual symbol distribution** calculated here is then not automatically the minimum average number of bits per symbol for the **entire dependent sequence**. For a stationary source, exploiting dependencies can make the so-called **entropy rate** smaller than the entropy of a symbol considered in isolation. An incorrectly estimated distribution or coding overhead may further reduce the compression actually achieved. Compression does not mean encryption, and a checksum or parity method does not automatically provide protection against deliberate manipulation.
