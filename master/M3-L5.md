# M3-L5 – Master: Mathematical statistics II – likelihood and asymptotic inference {#L5}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master’s-oriented advanced study following M1-L7, M3-L1 and M3-L4

## What is this about? {#S1}

A stochastic model often contains unknown parameters. For example, we have observed that a device fails on some days. How can we determine the underlying probability of failure from these observations? How much might the estimate vary with another dataset? And which conclusions are justified as the number of observations increases? Mathematical statistics investigates precisely these questions. This Master’s unit focuses on **likelihood, maximum likelihood estimation, Fisher information, asymptotic distributions and model criticism**.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Suppose a machine produces acceptable and defective parts. We do not know the long-term probability of a defect, but we observe data. For possible values of the unknown parameter, we calculate which ones explain the observed sample better. This rule of calculation is called **likelihood**. It answers a different question from the probability that the unknown parameter has a particular value: in classical likelihood, the dataset is held fixed and the model parameter is varied.

The more data we consider, the more accurate the parameter estimate often becomes. But **often** does not mean **always**: dependent observations, incorrect distributional assumptions or an unidentifiable model can render the usual standard formulae unusable.

## Likelihood: fixed data, variable parameter {#S4}

We first assume that the observations $X_{1},\ldots ,X_{n}$ are **independent** and **identically Bernoulli-distributed**. This means that each observation is either 1 (‘success’) or 0 (‘no success’), and each has the same unknown success probability $p$ with $0<p<1$. In statistics, ‘success’ is a neutral term: in defect statistics, 1 might actually denote a defect.

For a single observed value x from $\{0,1\}$, the probability is $p^x{(1-p)}^{1-x}$. For n independent observations, we multiply the factors. Since k of the values equal 1, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(p;x_1,\ldots,x_n)&=\prod_{i=1}^n p^{x_i}{(1-p)}^{1-x_i}\\
&=p^k{(1-p)}^{n-k}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here `∏` means ‘product of all factors’; the summation symbol from earlier lessons would add them instead. Read aloud: ‘The likelihood is p raised to the number of ones, multiplied by one minus p raised to the number of zeros.’ We use the probability of the **particular ordered sample**, not the probability of ‘exactly k successes’ in any order. As a factor independent of p, the binomial coefficient would not change the maximum of the likelihood.

**Numerical example:** We observe $(1,1,0,1,0)$. Thus $n=5$ and $k=3$. Two candidates give:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(p=0{,}5)&={(0{,}5)}^3\cdot{(0{,}5)}^2=0{,}03125\\
L(p=0{,}6)&={(0{,}6)}^3\cdot{(0{,}4)}^2=0{,}03456\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The second candidate makes **this exact ordered sample** somewhat more likely. The ratio does **not** imply that the true parameter equals 0,6 with probability 0,6. In the classical model, a parameter is not a random quantity as long as no prior distribution is introduced.

## Maximum likelihood step by step {#S5}

**Maximum likelihood estimation** (MLE) selects the parameter at which L is greatest. Since the natural logarithm is strictly increasing, we can maximise the more convenient **log-likelihood**:

$$
\ell(p)=\ln L(p)=k\ln(p)+(n-k)\ln(1-p)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘We add k times the logarithm of p and n minus k times the logarithm of one minus p.’ The derivative with respect to p is:

$$
\ell'(p)=\frac{k}{p}-\frac{n-k}{1-p}\text{.}
$$

For an interior maximum, we set the derivative to zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{k}{p}&=\frac{n-k}{1-p}\\
k(1-p)&=p(n-k)\\
k&=np\\
\hat p&=\frac{k}{n}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $k=3$ and $n=5$, we obtain $\hat{p}=\frac35=0{,}6$. This is the **relative frequency**. The fact that it corresponds to the MLE is not a general law of nature but a consequence of our specific Bernoulli model.

The second derivative for $0<k<n$ is:

$$
\ell''(p)=-\frac{k}{p^2}-\frac{n-k}{{(1-p)}^2}<0\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The log-likelihood is strictly concave here; the interior critical point we found is therefore the unique maximum.’ For $k=0$ or $k=n$, the maximum occurs at the boundary $p=0$ or $p=1$ respectively, if the admissible parameter space includes these boundaries. If the parameter space is **open**, the respective supremum is not attained. This boundary issue is important because standard asymptotic theorems often assume an **interior** true parameter.

## What does Fisher information mean? {#S6}

The **score function** is the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to the parameter. Under regularity conditions, Fisher information can be written as the expected value of the negative second derivative or as the variance of the score. Roughly speaking, it tells us how strongly the data distinguish between nearby parameter values.

For n independent Bernoulli observations, we obtain:

$$
I_n(p)=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Fisher information is the sample size divided by the product of the success and failure probabilities.’ In this model, the information grows **linearly with n**. For $n=100$ and $p=0{,}6$, it is:

$$
I_{100}(0{,}6)=\frac{100}{0{,}6\cdot0{,}4}=416{,}666\ldots\text{.}
$$

For an estimator that is **unbiased** under the usual regularity conditions, the Cramér–Rao bound gives the limit $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})\ge\frac1{I_n(\theta)}$. In the Bernoulli example, the sample frequency even has exactly:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}=\frac1{I_n(p)}\text{.}
$$

‘Exactly’ refers to the model assumptions, not to our actually knowing p. **Caution at the boundary:** For $p\to 0$ or $p\to 1$, the expression $\frac1{p(1-p)}$ grows without bound. However, this does not imply a reliable normal approximation directly at $p=0$ or $p=1$: the usual interior parameter and regularity assumptions are violated there. For vector-valued parameters, the information becomes a matrix. In poorly identifiable models, this matrix can be singular; an algorithm may then fail to find a unique or stable parameter estimate.

## Asymptotically normal: many data, a limiting statement {#S7}

We distinguish **consistency** from **asymptotic normality**. Consistency means that the estimator approaches the true parameter as the sample grows. For independent, identically Bernoulli-distributed observations, this follows for $\hat{p}$ from the law of large numbers.

For a fixed true parameter $0<p<1$, the central limit theorem additionally gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\sqrt n\cdot(\hat p-p)}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}\xrightarrow{\text{in distribution}}\mathcal N(0,1)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘As n increases, the normalised difference between the estimate and the true parameter behaves approximately like a standard normal distribution.’ Here the arrow denotes **specifically convergence in distribution**, not pointwise convergence of a particular numerical value. Under regularity assumptions, many other maximum likelihood estimators also have such an asymptotic normal distribution, often with the inverse Fisher information as the variance scale.

**Important limitation:** The theorem does not claim an exact normal distribution for every finite sample. In particular, for very small n or parameters close to 0 and 1, the normal approximation can be poor.

## An approximate 95 per cent confidence interval {#S8}

We now have $n=100$ independent observations, of which $k=60$ are ones. Thus $\hat{p}=0{,}6$. We calculate the **estimated standard error** by substituting the estimate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)&=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{0{,}6\cdot0{,}4}{100}}\\
&=\sqrt{0{,}0024}\approx0{,}0490\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The classical **Wald interval** uses approximately 1,96 standard errors on either side:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat p\pm1{,}96\cdot\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)
&=0{,}6\pm1{,}96\cdot0{,}0490\\
&\approx[0{,}504\,;\,0{,}696]\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Under the model of 100 independent Bernoulli observations, the approximate 95 per cent confidence interval ranges from about 50,4 to 69,6 per cent.’ The **95 per cent** describes the long-term coverage of the repeatedly calculated procedure under its assumptions – **not** a 95 per cent probability, after the observations, that the fixed parameter lies in this exact interval.

The Wald interval has known weaknesses for small samples and probabilities near the boundary. A **Wilson interval** or an appropriate exact procedure may be better in such cases. Even for $n=100$, the 95 per cent figure is only approximate, not guaranteed to be exact.

### Cross-check: the Wald interval at the boundary {#abschnitt-9}

Instead of 100, imagine **ten independent Bernoulli observations without a single success** ($n=10$, $k=0$). The Wald formula then gives $\hat{p}=0$, $\mathrm{SE}=0$ and thus the apparently certain interval $[0;0]$. This is **not** a useful expression of the actual sampling uncertainty. A Wilson interval with the usual 1,96 factor gives approximately the following for the same data:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Wilson lower bound}&=0\\
\text{Wilson upper bound}&=\frac{{(1{,}96)}^2}{10+{(1{,}96)}^2}\\
&\approx0{,}278\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘Ten observed zeros do not prove that the unknown success probability is exactly zero.’ Wilson intervals likewise do not guarantee precisely 95 per cent coverage for every finite Bernoulli distribution. The comparison shows why a numerically narrower interval does **not automatically** support a better conclusion.

## Likelihood ratio, hypothesis and misinterpretation {#S9}

To compare two candidate parameter values, we can consider the likelihood ratio, for example. For the null hypothesis $p=p_{0}$, a common test statistic is:

$$
D=2\left(\ell(\hat p)-\ell(p_0)\right)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: ‘We take twice the difference between the best possible log-likelihood and the log-likelihood under the null hypothesis.’ Under suitable regularity conditions and when the null hypothesis is true, D approaches a chi-squared distribution; for a **single** regular parameter, one degree of freedom is commonly used. At boundary points, in unidentifiable models or with dependent data, this limiting distribution may be different.

### Worked model comparison {#abschnitt-11}

In the example with $n=100$ and $k=60$, we test $p_{0}=0{,}5$ against a freely estimated success probability. For the same ordered sample, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&=2\cdot\left[60\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{0{,}6}{0{,}5}\right)+40\cdot\ln\!\left(\frac{0{,}4}{0{,}5}\right)\right]\\
&\approx4{,}0271\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under the **asymptotic** chi-squared reference distribution with one degree of freedom, this corresponds to a p-value of approximately **0,0448**. In contrast, a directly calculated **two-sided exact binomial test** (for $p_{0}=0{,}5$, we sum the probabilities of at most 40 or at least 60 successes) gives approximately **0,0569**. These procedures have different finite-sample calibrations; they must not be presented as identical tests that necessarily produce identical p-values. The example reveals the difference between a limiting distribution approximation and an exact model calculation.

A **p-value** is a conditional statement about the distribution of a test statistic **under the null hypothesis**. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Neither a small p-value nor a narrow confidence interval proves that the original model family is correct.

## What dependence and model errors change {#S10}

M3-L4 introduced processes with temporal dependence. If we take 100 consecutive measurements of the state of the same machine, we cannot treat them as 100 independent Bernoulli trials simply because of the number ‘100’. With positive temporal correlation, the **effective sample size** is often smaller. Uncertainty must then be calculated using suitable process models, time series methods or other methods that accommodate dependence.

**Non-identifiability:** If two different parameters generate the same distribution of observations, even infinitely many observations cannot distinguish them in principle. **Model misspecification:** If the data-generating process and assumed distribution do not agree, an MLE may converge to a substitute parameter rather than the desired physical quantity. **Selection bias:** If only successful experiments are reported, even a formally correct confidence interval does not protect against this bias.

**Alternative Bayesian perspective:** In a Bayesian model, the parameter itself is assigned a prior distribution. Together with the likelihood, it gives a posterior distribution. A **credible region** (credible interval) therefore has a different interpretation from a frequentist confidence interval. These two statements should not be conflated in language.

## What is it useful for? {#S11}

- **AI evaluation:** Report error rates and performance differences with uncertainty rather than just point scores.
- **Robotics:** Estimate transition, measurement noise and sensor models from data.
- **Maintenance and manufacturing:** Quantify failure and defect parameters and take uncertainty into account when making decisions.
- **Research and software benchmarks:** Formulate hypotheses, sampling assumptions and confidence intervals transparently.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S12}

The calculations with $\hat{p}=\frac{k}{n}$, Fisher information and the Wald interval are based on a sample model of **independent, identically Bernoulli-distributed observations**. General MLE theory requires, among other things, identifiability and suitable smoothness, as well as conditions on the location of the parameter. Asymptotic results are limiting statements; they replace neither sample diagnostics nor subject-specific plausibility. Taking multiple testing, predefined analyses and data collection conditions into account is part of sound application.

## Key points {#S14}

Likelihood evaluates model parameters **for fixed data**. The maximum likelihood estimator maximises this function, not the probability of a hypothesis. A standard error describes the sampling variation of the estimation procedure; asymptotic distribution theorems require verifiable assumptions. Statistical accuracy is no substitute for choosing the right model.
