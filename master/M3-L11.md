# M3-L11 – Master's: Inverse problems, regularisation and mathematical AI methods {#L11}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master's-oriented advanced study following M1-L6 (linear algebra), M3-L2 (operators), M3-L5 (statistical estimation), M3-L6 (convex optimisation), M3-L7 (singular values), M3-L10 (Fourier) and M2-L11 (AI models)

## What is this about? {#S1}

In a **forward problem**, the cause is known and its observable effect is calculated. For example, a sensor measures a voltage produced by an object. In an **inverse problem**, only the effect is known and the aim is to reconstruct the unknown cause. This can be surprisingly difficult: two different causes may produce the same measurement, or a tiny measurement error may become enormous during reconstruction. **Regularisation** therefore supplements computational reconstruction with justified prior assumptions or constraints. There are close mathematical links to machine learning: an AI model, too, is intended to infer reliable rules from incomplete, noisy observations.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

A camera blurs a scene. If we know how the blur arises, we might try simply reversing it. However, blurring removes fine details in particular, precisely where sensor noise and rounding errors often dominate. A mathematically correct inversion of the idealised equation can therefore produce a practically unusable image. We need a compromise between **data fidelity** and **plausibility of the reconstruction**. Such a compromise is neither proof that the reconstruction is correct nor automatically a statistically calibrated measure of uncertainty.

## Forward model and the inverse question {#S4}

A simple linear measurement model is:

$$
b=A\,x+\varepsilon\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “The measured data vector b is the effect of matrix A on the unknown cause x, plus an error vector epsilon.” In image reconstruction, x contains the unknown image values, A represents the blurring and b contains the recorded pixels. In robotics and navigation, x contains position or material parameters, for example, while A represents the linearised sensor model.

The forward direction, “given x, calculate b”, may be easy even when the reverse direction, “given b, reconstruct x”, is practically impossible. This affects not only large matrices but even a tiny system with two unknowns.

## When is a mathematical problem well-posed? {#S5}

A classical **well-posed problem** in Hadamard's sense has three properties: (1) a solution exists; (2) it is unique; (3) it depends continuously on the data, so small changes in the data produce small changes in the solution. An inverse problem can lose any of these properties.

- **Existence:** An idealised model may not reproduce noisy measurement data exactly. In that case, there is no x with Ax=b.
- **Uniqueness:** If a non-zero vector v lies in the null space of A, then x and $x+v$ have the same effect, since $A(x+v)=Ax+Av=Ax$.
- **Stability:** Even if A is invertible, very small singular values can amplify a small perturbation of the data enormously.

These three difficulties are distinct. More computing power does not by itself recover information lost in a null space. And a numerically tiny residual $\Vert Ax-b\Vert _{2}$ does not yet mean that x resembles the actual cause.

## Fully worked example: hundredfold error amplification {#S6}

We choose a diagonal measurement matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0{,}01\end{bmatrix}\\
x_{\mathrm{wahr}}&=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first measurement direction is transmitted unchanged; the second is attenuated by a factor of one hundredth. Without error, the measurement would be:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_{\mathrm{ideal}}&=Ax_{\mathrm{wahr}}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0{,}01\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now only the second measured value increases from $0{,}01$ to $0{,}02$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_{\mathrm{gemessen}}&=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0{,}02\end{bmatrix}\\
\varepsilon&=b_{\mathrm{gemessen}}-b_{\mathrm{ideal}}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0{,}01\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The magnitude of the measurement error is only $\Vert \varepsilon \Vert _{2}=0{,}01$. If we **naively invert** the diagonal matrix, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{\mathrm{naiv}}&=A^{-1}b_{\mathrm{gemessen}}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&100\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2\\0{,}02\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Although the measurement has changed by only one hundredth, the second reconstructed unknown changes by a whole unit.” The reconstruction error has length one, and the data error has length one hundredth; in this direction the **error amplification is exactly one hundred**. The two-norm condition number of A is also one hundred: the largest singular value, one, divided by the smallest singular value, $0{,}01$.

## Tikhonov regularisation: data fidelity versus large parameters {#S7}

For real matrices and the reference value $x_{0}=0$, we define the Tikhonov objective function:

$$
J_{\lambda}(x)=\lVert Ax-b\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert x\rVert_2^2,\qquad\text{with }\lambda>0\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “We minimise the squared difference between modelled and measured data, plus lambda times the squared norm of the reconstruction vector.” The first term demands data fidelity; the second penalises very large parameters. The parameter $\lambda$ determines how strongly the solution is shifted away from the measurement towards small values.

Differentiating $J_\lambda$ with respect to x and setting the gradient equal to zero gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla J_\lambda(x)&=2A^\top(Ax-b)+2\lambda x=0,\\
(A^\top A+\lambda I)x_\lambda&=A^\top b,\\
x_\lambda&={(A^\top A+\lambda I)}^{-1}A^\top b\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The minimum satisfies a linear system of equations formed from the original measurement matrix and an identity matrix weighted by lambda.” In finite-dimensional spaces and for **$\lambda$\>0**, $A^{\top}A+\lambda I$ is always symmetric positive definite and hence invertible; there is exactly one minimiser. For complex or infinite-dimensional spaces, the adjoint $A^*$ must be used instead of $A^{\top}$, and the operator conditions must be checked.

### The complete numerical example with regularisation {#abschnitt-8}

For the matrix above, we deliberately choose $\lambda =0{,}0001$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top A+\lambda I&=\begin{bmatrix}1{,}0001&0\\0&0{,}0002\end{bmatrix},\\
A^\top b&=\begin{bmatrix}2\\0{,}0002\end{bmatrix}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

We solve each row separately:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{\lambda,1}&=\frac{2}{1{,}0001}\approx1{,}99980002,\\
x_{\lambda,2}&=\frac{0{,}0002}{0{,}0002}=1\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The first parameter is barely changed; the second returns from the unstable value two to one.” In **this deliberately chosen example**, the regularised second component happens to equal the true value exactly. This is **not a general guarantee** of the Tikhonov method: in real inverse problems, we do not normally know the true parameter, and regularised solutions can also be biased or incorrect.

If we choose a larger $\lambda$, both components of the solution are pushed more strongly towards the zero vector; if we choose $\lambda$ too small, the noise problem returns. Choosing $\lambda$ is a mathematical and empirical problem in its own right, for example using known noise bounds, validation data or specific selection criteria.

## Singular values explain the mechanism {#S8}

The singular value decomposition $A=U\Sigma V^{\top}$ was introduced in M3-L7. For a singular value $\sigma$, naive inversion has the factor $\frac1\sigma$. A small singular value therefore greatly amplifies measurement noise. Tikhonov replaces this factor in the corresponding direction with:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\sigma}{\sigma^2+\lambda}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Instead of dividing by sigma, we multiply by sigma and divide by sigma squared plus lambda.” For $\sigma =0{,}01$ and $\lambda =0{,}0001$, the factor is $\frac{0{,}01}{0{,}0002}=50$ rather than one hundred. For very small $\sigma$, it approaches zero at fixed $\lambda$; precisely the unstable directions are attenuated. The filter $\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+\lambda}$ describes the effect on a **true** singular vector coefficient, so systematic **bias** may arise. Noise reduction thus comes at the cost of information loss.

An alternative method is the **truncated singular value decomposition**: only singular values above a threshold are inverted. This, too, is regularisation, with a different type of filter and information loss. The real question is not “which formula inverts A?” but “which information can reliably be identified in b?”

## Structural knowledge rather than just small numbers {#S9}

Sometimes a small parameter vector is not a plausible physical assumption. In a noisy temperature curve, for instance, we might prefer to penalise large **fluctuations** rather than a high but constant temperature. In that case, we use an operator L and a reference value $x_{0}$:

$$
J_{\lambda}(x)=\lVert Ax-b\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert L(x-x_0)\rVert_2^2\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “We not only reward agreement with the data, but also favour solutions whose structure, as measured by L, is close to our reference model.” L can, for example, subtract neighbouring values from one another so that abrupt fluctuations are penalised. But if $\ker (A)$ and $\ker (L)$ share a non-zero vector, the solution may **not be unique** despite $\lambda$\>0. The simple uniqueness result from the preceding section applies directly only for $L=I$.

Other regularisers, such as the absolute values of the parameters or **total variation**, can also be used; the optimisation is then not always smooth. Regularisation encodes **assumptions**, not objectively indisputable truth.

### Countercheck: the general penalty norm does not make every solution unique {#abschnitt-11}

The condition $\lambda >0$ is **not** sufficient if the penalty operator L itself fails to detect certain directions. A two-dimensional counterexample is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=[1\quad0],\quad L=[1\quad0],\quad b=[2],\\
x_0&={[0,0]}^\top,\quad\lambda=1\text{.}\\
J(x_1,x_2)&={(x_1-2)}^2+x_1^2\\
&=2{(x_1-1)}^2+2\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Only the first component is measured and penalised; the second remains completely invisible.” J has the same minimum value of 2 for **every** $x_{2}\in \mathbb{R}$ at $x_{1}=1$. Thus, for example, $(1,0)$ and $(1,100)$ both have the same optimal objective value. The common null direction $(0,1)$ lies in both $\ker (A)$ and $\ker (L)$. For finite-dimensional real matrices, $\ker (A)\cap \ker (L)=\{0\}$ is precisely the condition for $A^{\top}A+\lambda L^{\top}L$ to be positive definite for $\lambda >0$. The generalised quadratic Tikhonov solution is then unique. Without this condition, there may be several minimisers despite positive $\lambda$.

### Countercheck: regularisation produces bias even without measurement noise {#abschnitt-12}

In Section 4, the true vector was $x_{\mathrm{wahr}}={(2,1)}^\top$ and the weak measurement component with noise was $0{,}02$. With the same $\lambda$ of $0{,}0001$, but **without** measurement error ($b_{\mathrm{ideal}}=(2;0{,}01)$), the regulariser instead gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{\lambda,1}&=\frac{2}{1{,}0001}\approx1{,}99980002,\\
x_{\lambda,2}&=\frac{0{,}01\cdot0{,}01}{{(0{,}01)}^2+0{,}0001}=0{,}5\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “A regulariser can improve a noisy component while pulling an error-free measured component away from the true value.” For the second component, the true value is 1, whereas the new reconstruction is only 0.5. The exact agreement in the first example was **coincidental**, not evidence of generally error-free reconstruction.

## Statistical interpretation: posterior rather than certainty {#S10}

A deterministic reconstruction initially yields a numerical vector. If we want to quantify uncertainty, we need a **probability model**. Let us explicitly assume:

1.  The measurement is linear: $b=Ax+\varepsilon$.
2.  The noise components are independent and normally distributed with mean zero and variance $\sigma ^{2}$.
3.  Before the measurement, we use a normally distributed parameter prior $x\sim N(0,\tau^2 I)$.
4.  The prior and noise are independent; A and $\sigma$, $\tau$ are assumed known.

Bayes' formula gives the **negative logarithm of the posterior density**, up to an additive constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log p(x\mid b)
&=\frac1{2\sigma^2}\lVert Ax-b\rVert_2^2+
  \frac1{2\tau^2}\lVert x\rVert_2^2+\text{constant}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Large deviations from the measured value and from prior knowledge are each penalised quadratically; the variances determine their weights.” Multiplication by $2\sigma ^{2}$ does not change the minimiser. Therefore, the **MAP method** corresponds exactly to Tikhonov regularisation with:

$$
\lambda=\frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “Lambda here is the ratio of measurement noise variance to prior variance.” This is a specific justification for a parameter value, but only if the entire probabilistic model is appropriate. The MAP point is not the whole posterior distribution. Under these linear Gaussian assumptions, the posterior covariance is:

$$
C_{\mathrm{post}}=\left(\frac{A^{\mathrm{T}}A}{\sigma^2}+\frac I{\tau^2}\right)^{-1}\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “Uncertainty arises from the sum of the measurement information and the information from prior knowledge.” Distribution shifts, undetected systematic errors and incorrect priors can considerably distort this uncertainty. A Bayesian numerical result is never independent of its assumptions.

## Connection to AI: learning is not the same as reconstruction {#S11}

In supervised learning, a model selects parameters from training data that make a loss function small. Additional regularisation by a penalty term such as $\lambda{\Vert x\Vert}^2$ can also help to prevent excessively large weights. **The two are mathematically related, but not the same:** in an inverse problem, the forward operator A is often physically given; in a neural network, the mapping itself is learned from examples and is usually **nonlinear and overparameterised**.

The three distinct questions are:

- **Identifiability:** Can different parameters explain the same observations?
- **Reconstruction:** Which parameters fit the measurement model and prior assumptions?
- **Generalisation:** Does the trained model also work with new, independent data from the intended application domain?

A small **training error** does not answer the last question. Suitable separate validation and test data are needed; potential data leakage must be checked and changes in the data distribution taken into account. Large neural networks can also have multiple parameter configurations with practically identical predictions. Unique reconstruction of the “true weights” is therefore rarely a meaningful objective.

## Iterative methods and implicit regularisation {#S12}

If the matrix is too large for direct inversion, we can start with the **Landweber step** for the pure quadratic data error:

$$
x_{k+1}=x_k-\eta A^{\mathrm{T}}(Ax_k-b)\text{.}
$$

Read aloud: “From the current estimate, we subtract a step size multiplied by the back-projected measurement discrepancy.” This is gradient descent for **half** the squared data discrepancy $0{,}5{\Vert Ax-b\Vert}_2^2$. For finite-dimensional A and $0<\eta<\frac2{{\Vert A\Vert}_2^2}$, the step size lies within the usual convergence range of the quadratic problem. For ill-posed problems, **early stopping** can be preferable to further iterations, because late steps can reproduce unstable directions and thus measurement noise more strongly. “More iterations” does not automatically mean “more truth”.

## What is it useful for? {#S13}

In **medical imaging** or computed tomography, a spatial cause is reconstructed from indirect projections. In **astronomy and image processing**, a blurred, noisy signal is reconstructed. In **robotics and sensor fusion**, the aim is to determine states or material parameters from partial measurements. In **AI**, this perspective helps distinguish training loss, regularisation assumptions, model identification and genuine predictive performance.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S14}

- A small residual does not prove correct reconstruction; where null spaces exist, the cause is not fully identifiable.
- $\lambda >0$ guarantees a unique minimum for the **standard Tikhonov form with $L=I$ in finite dimensions**; arbitrary operators and penalty operators require additional conditions.
- The appropriate regulariser depends on the application objective. Smoothness, sparsity and small norms are different assumptions.
- The posterior formulae apply only under the explicitly stated **linear Gaussian assumptions** with fixed variances.
- A Bayesian uncertainty estimate does not automatically account for model errors, unnoticed disturbances or distribution shifts.
- The examples use linear operators. Nonlinear inverse problems may have local minima, ambiguities and additional sensitivities.
- AI generalisation does not follow solely from a small training error or the use of a regulariser.
