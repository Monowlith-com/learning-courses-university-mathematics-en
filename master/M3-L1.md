# M3-L1 – Master: Measure theory II and convergence theorems {#L1}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master’s-oriented advanced study following M1-L2, M1-L3, M1-L7 and M2-L7

## What is this about? {#S1}

With finite sums, we can often change the order without noticing any difference. With infinitely many terms or increasingly fine approximations, the same step may be invalid. Measure theory provides precise rules for when limits and integrals may be interchanged. It is the foundation of modern probability theory, scientific simulation and much of functional analysis. The central question is not simply ‘How do I integrate?’, but **‘Why am I allowed to move this limit inside the integral?’**

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a sequence of sensor signals with increasingly fine resolution. We want to know whether the measured total energy in the limit agrees with the energy of the limiting signal. The fact that the signals approach a value at each individual instant is not sufficient: very narrow and simultaneously very high spikes can retain a contribution to the integral. Two important theorems, **monotone convergence** and **dominated convergence**, specify different sufficient conditions.

## New symbols – read these first {#S3}

| $1_A(x)$ | ‘indicator of A at x’ | One in A, zero outside A | A probability itself |
| `almost everywhere` / `a.e.` | ‘almost everywhere’ | Except on a set of measure zero | ‘At every point’ |

| $L^{1}(\Omega )$ | ‘L one of omega’ | Space of integrable functions up to equality almost everywhere | The letter l as the numeral one |
| `∑` | ‘sum over’ | Addition over an index set | An integral |

A **measure space** consists of $(\Omega ,\mathcal{A},\mu )$: $\Omega$ is the set of points, $\mathcal A$ specifies the measurable sets, and $\mu$ specifies their size. On an interval, for example, length is a measure. The condition ‘almost everywhere’ means that exceptions are permitted provided that the exceptional set has measure zero. Such exceptions may exist mathematically despite having measure zero.

## Measurability and integrability {#S4}

A function is measurable if its values are compatible with the measurable sets; one common formulation requires, for example, that for each real number a the set $\{x\in \Omega  : f(x)>a\}$ be measurable. We do not need to construct complicated sigma-algebras for the following examples; continuous functions on intervals are measurable.

For a non-negative measurable function, the Lebesgue integral may be infinite. A signed function is **Lebesgue integrable** if the integral of its absolute value is finite:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_\Omega |f|\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The integral over omega of the absolute value of f with respect to the measure mu is less than infinity.’ Only this condition allows us to treat the integral of a general signed function as a finite number. In $L^{1}(\Omega )$, we consider such functions, treating functions that are equal almost everywhere as the same element.

## Monotone convergence theorem {#S5}

In a common formulation, Beppo Levi’s theorem states that if all $f_{n}$ are measurable and non-negative, and $f_{n}(x)$ is non-decreasing as n increases and converges to $f(x)$ for almost every x, then the following holds even for potentially infinite integrals:

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f_n\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
$$

Read aloud: ‘The limit of the integrals of f sub n is the integral of the limiting function f.’ **Conditions:** non-negative measurable functions and monotone approximation from below. An arbitrary sequence does not have this guarantee.

**Fully explained example:** For n at least two, we define on $[0,1]$ an indicator function $f_{n}(x)$ that equals one on the interval $\left[0,1-\frac1n\right]$ and zero elsewhere. For a fixed x less than one, x belongs to this interval once n is sufficiently large; thus $f_{n}(x)$ tends to one. At the single point $x=1$, the value remains zero, but this point has length measure zero. The integral at each stage is the length of the interval:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=1-\frac1n\longrightarrow 1\\
\int_0^1 1\,\mathrm{d}x&=1
\end{aligned}
$$

The equality of the limits is intuitive here, and the theorem justifies it for much more complicated monotone sequences as well. The example uses only elementary interval lengths, not hidden tricks involving limits.

## Dominated convergence theorem {#S6}

A sequence need not be monotone. Another theorem, **dominated convergence**, permits the interchange under stronger control of the magnitudes: suppose $f_{n}$ is measurable, converges to f almost everywhere, and there is **one single** integrable function g with $|f_{n}(x)|\le g(x)$ for all n and almost every x. Then f is integrable, and:

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f_n\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu
$$

Read aloud: ‘If all functions in the sequence are controlled by the same integrable dominating function and converge almost everywhere, then their integrals also converge to the integral of the limiting function.’ **A different bound for each n is not sufficient.**

**Worked example:** On $[0,1]$, let $f_{n}(x)=x^n$. For every x in the interval, $0\le x^n\le 1$; the constant function $g(x)=1$ is integrable. For every $0\le x<1$, $x^n$ tends to zero, while at the point $x=1$ the value remains one. The sequence therefore converges almost everywhere to the zero function. The integrals can be calculated independently:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 x^n\,\mathrm{d}x&=\left[\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}\right]_0^1=\frac1{n+1}\longrightarrow0\\
\int_0^1 0\,\mathrm{d}x&=0
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The integral of x to the power n is the fraction one over n plus one, and decreases towards zero.’ The convergence theorem is justified here by the common bound of one; the direct calculation confirms the result.

## Counterexample: a pointwise limit alone is not sufficient {#S7}

Now consider on $[0,1]$ the sequence $h_{n}(x)=n$ if $0<x<\frac1n$, and $h_{n}(x)=0$ otherwise. For every fixed point x greater than zero, the narrow interval disappears for all sufficiently large n. At $x=0$, the value is always zero. Thus $h_{n}(x)$ tends to zero **at every point**. But:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 h_n(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\text{height}\times\text{width}=n\cdot\frac1n=1\\
\int_0^1\lim_{n\to\infty}h_n(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_0^1 0\,\mathrm{d}x=0
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The areas of all the tall, narrow rectangles remain one, although their height is concentrated in an increasingly narrow region and every fixed point eventually has the value zero.’ There is no common integrable dominating function for this sequence. This can even be justified: suppose $g$ were a non-negative integrable function with $g(x)\ge h_{n}(x)$ for all n outside a common null set. On each of the pairwise disjoint intervals $\left(\frac{1}{n+1},\frac1n\right)$, we have $h_{n}(x)=n$. Hence $g(x)\ge n$ would have to hold there. It follows for every $N\ge 2$ that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&\ge\sum_{n=1}^{N} n\cdot\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{n+1}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The area under such a common bound would be at least the sum of one over n plus one for all the subintervals considered.’ These partial sums grow without a finite bound (harmonic series, M1-L2). Thus $g$ would not be integrable – a contradiction. Claiming that the two limiting processes could be interchanged would be incorrect. Precisely such effects are important in numerical simulations with increasingly steep spikes.

## Fubini and Tonelli: order of integration {#S8}

For two variables, integrals are often evaluated successively. **Tonelli’s theorem** permits this for non-negative measurable functions even when the integrals become infinite. **Fubini’s theorem** permits the order of integration to be changed under absolute integrability, with a finite result. Here we are discussing the usual **$\sigma$-finite measure spaces** with the corresponding product measure; on more general measure spaces, the conditions for constructing the product measure must be checked separately. ‘Sigma-finite’ means that the space can be written as a countable union of sets of finite measure. Without the necessary conditions in each case, rearrangements can be dangerous.

For a simple rectangular example, consider $f(x,y)=x+2y$ with $0\le x\le 1$ and $0\le y\le 2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\left(\int_0^2(x+2y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathrm{d}x
&=\int_0^1(2x+4)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\left[x^2+4x\right]_0^1\\
&=5
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘First we integrate with respect to y while keeping x fixed, and then we integrate the remaining expression with respect to x.’ Reversing the order also gives five. The function in this example is continuous on a compact rectangle; the conditions for changing the order are therefore satisfied here.

### Additional sufficient condition: convergence in $L^1$ {#abschnitt-9}

For integrable functions, the difference between the integrals satisfies the following bound:

$$
\left\lvert\int_{\Omega} f_n\,\mathrm{d}\mu-\int_{\Omega} f\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rvert\leq\int_{\Omega}\lvert f_n-f\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The absolute value of the difference between the two integrals is at most the integral of the absolute value of the difference between the functions.’ If the right-hand side tends to zero, we call this **convergence in $L^1$**. The integrals then converge automatically. This is a different, directly verifiable condition from pointwise convergence.

**Mini-example:** On $[0,1]$, consider $f_n(x)=\frac{x}{n}$ and $f(x)=0$ for $n\ge 1$. The functions are integrable, and:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1|f_n(x)-0|\,\mathrm{d}x&=\int_0^1\frac xn\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac1{2n}\longrightarrow0\\
\int_0^1 f_n(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\frac1{2n}\longrightarrow0=\int_0^1 f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
\end{aligned}
$$

Here the sequence even converges **uniformly**, because the maximum distance is $\frac1n$. On a measure space of finite total measure, uniform convergence of integrable functions is also sufficient: the difference between the integrals is at most $\mu (\Omega )$ times the maximum distance between the functions. On spaces of infinite total measure, this finite bound cannot be adopted without checking the conditions.

## Connection to probability and function spaces {#S9}

In probability theory, $\mu$ often corresponds to the probability measure $P$. The expected value of an integrable random variable is its integral with respect to P. Under appropriate bounds, dominated convergence then justifies the transition from the expected values of approximating random variables to the expected value of the limiting model.

In numerical mathematics and AI, the question is similar: when a sequence of functions converges to a model, can we also carry costs, probabilities or error measures through the limiting process? **Pointwise convergence, norm convergence and almost sure convergence** are different statements; none should be reinterpreted as another without additional arguments.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

- In **Monte Carlo methods**, it helps to distinguish clearly between function, measure and expected value.
- In **digital signal models**, it prevents incorrect conclusions based on pointwise approximation and global signal energy.
- In **PDEs and numerical simulation**, the controlled interchange of limits and integrals is one step towards justifying approximation methods.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

We have stated central theorems and checked simple examples, not given their complete proofs on general measure spaces. This applies in particular to the construction of the Lebesgue integral and the measure-theoretic conditions of Fubini’s theorem. The existence of a pointwise limit alone does **not** imply that the interchange is valid. Dominated convergence does not prove that a finite-precision numerical implementation is stable either.
