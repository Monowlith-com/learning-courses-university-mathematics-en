# M2-L7 – Functional analysis and measure theory {#L7}

**Course area:** M2 – Advanced Bachelor’s studies / transition to Master’s studies

**Level:** Bachelor’s / bridge to Master’s studies

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** University – further advanced studies

## What is this about? {#S1}

So far, we have mainly considered individual numbers and vectors. However, **entire functions** can also be treated as mathematical objects: we can add functions, measure the differences between them and ask whether a sequence of functions converges to another function. **Functional analysis** studies such spaces of functions and the mappings between them. **Measure theory** answers a different fundamental question: what does the ‘size’ of a highly complicated set mean, and how can we define integration precisely on that basis? Both fields are among the mathematical foundations of modern differential equations, probability theory and signal processing.

This lesson extends M1-L2 (convergence), M1-L3 (integration), M1-L5 (vector spaces), M1-L7 (probability) and M2-L4 (topological spaces). The aim is to **read and understand the concepts and formulas**, not to construct the entire theory.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

An ordinary vector may have three entries – for example, the colour values of a pixel. A function, by contrast, can specify a measurement **at every** instant throughout an interval. We can therefore think of a function as a kind of vector with a continuum of coordinates. Although this comparison is not literally correct in every respect, it explains why function spaces are related to vector spaces.

We encounter a similar problem when measuring: a rectangle has a simple area, but some sets of points are so irregular that familiar school geometry is insufficient. Measure theory allows us to assign a consistent numerical size to suitable sets. Examples of such values are length, area, volume or – after appropriate normalisation – probability.

## Measure theory: size as a mathematical assignment {#S4}

A **measure** is a rule that assigns a non-negative size to suitable sets. For length on the real number line, for example, we want the interval from zero to two to have length two and two non-overlapping intervals together to have the sum of their lengths.

For disjoint sets – that is, sets with no elements in common – a central principle is **countable additivity**: the size of a finite or countably infinite union is the sum of the individual sizes. A measure is not necessarily defined on **every conceivable subset**, but on a suitable family of sets called a *sigma-algebra*. A sigma-algebra contains the underlying set and is closed under complementation and countable unions. A measure satisfies $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and countable additivity on **pairwise disjoint** measurable sets; its values may also be infinite. Thus, on an infinite underlying set, the total size need not be finite.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu([0,2])&=2\quad\text{(ordinary length measure)}\\
\mu([0,1])+\mu([1,2])&=1+1=2
\end{aligned}
$$

**Spoken:** ‘Mu of the closed interval from zero to two is two. Each of the two subintervals has length one.’ Their common boundary consists only of the point one, whose **length measure is zero**. The equality of sums shown here is therefore correct, even though the two closed subintervals are not disjoint: the overlap has measure zero. To apply the additivity rule directly to disjoint sets, we can use $[0,1]$ and $(1,2]$: their union is $[0,2]$ and their lengths are one and one.

**Important:** An individual point does belong to the set of numbers, even though its length is zero. *Measure zero* does not mean *non-existent*.

## Lebesgue integration: more than rectangles under a graph {#S5}

At school, integration was introduced using small strips of area under a curve. The **Lebesgue integral** generalises summation over sets to which a measure has been assigned. For ordinary length measure, the familiar integral and the Lebesgue integral agree for sufficiently well-behaved functions.

$$
\int_0^1x\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{2}
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The integral from zero to one of x with respect to x is one half.’ Here, $\mathrm{d}x$ denotes integration with respect to the coordinate $x$, corresponding to ordinary length measure. In generalised notation, the same expression can be described as ‘the integral of f with respect to mu’ if $f(x)=x$ and $\mu$ is length measure.

The strength of measure theory becomes apparent when a function has a highly irregular shape, or when limiting processes and integration must be carefully coordinated. Clear conditions are required; arbitrary infinite processes cannot simply be interchanged.

### Mini-example: why limits are difficult in integration {#abschnitt-6}

For every positive integer $n$, consider a function $f_n$ on the interval from zero to one. On the very narrow section from zero to $\frac{1}{n}$, it equals $n$, and it is zero elsewhere. For each of these functions, the area is:

$$
\text{width}\cdot\text{height}=\frac1n\cdot n=1
$$

**Spoken:** ‘One divided by n times n is one.’ As $n$ increases, the narrow spike becomes taller but narrower. At **every fixed point x greater than zero**, the function values eventually become zero. This means that the sequence of functions approaches the zero function almost everywhere, yet every individual integral remains one.

At the point zero itself, the function value grows without bound. However, this individual point has length measure zero, so we speak of **almost-everywhere convergence**. In the measure-theoretic sense, the limiting function is zero almost everywhere and its integral is zero, whereas the integrals of the individual functions are always one. At the explicitly excluded point $x=0$, there is **no pointwise convergence to zero**: here, $f_n(0)=n$.

**Concrete consequence:** The sequence therefore converges to zero almost everywhere, but **not in $L^{1}$ distance**: $\int_0^1\lvert f_n(x)-0\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x=1$ for every $n$. Nor is there a single integrable dominating function that bounds the absolute values of all $f_n$ almost everywhere; otherwise, the dominated convergence theorem would contradict the observed integral values.

**Lesson:** Pointwise convergence almost everywhere does **not automatically** imply that a limit and integration may be interchanged. There are theorems with additional conditions for this, such as the *dominated convergence theorem*. A typical requirement is that the absolute values of all the functions are bounded above by **the same integrable function**.

## Function spaces: treating entire signals like vectors {#S6}

In simplified terms, the space $L^{1}$ contains functions whose absolute value is integrable. The space $L^{2}$ contains functions whose **squared absolute value** is integrable. Technically, we identify functions that differ only on a set of measure zero: two such functions are treated as the same element of the $L^{2}$ space.

The essential formula for the $L^{2}$ norm on an interval is:

$$
\lVert f\rVert_2=\sqrt{\int_a^b\lvert f(x)\rvert^2\,\mathrm{d}x}
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The L two norm of f is the square root of the integral from a to b of the absolute value of f of x squared, with respect to x.’ The result is a **non-negative number** describing how large the function is over the entire interval in this sense.

### Mini-example: a constant signal {#abschnitt-8}

A signal has a constant value of three over two seconds. Its $L^{2}$ norm on the interval $[0,2]$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=3\\
\lVert f\rVert_2&=\sqrt{\int_0^2 9\,\mathrm{d}x}\\
&=\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2\approx4{,}24
\end{aligned}
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The norm is the square root of nine times two, or three times the square root of two.’ This example shows that a norm summarises the size of an **entire time history**, rather than measuring an arbitrary instantaneous value. If the signal lasts for a shorter or longer period, the norm changes. For a signal quantity measured in volts and a time axis in seconds, this norm has the unit $\mathrm{V}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{s}}$, not simply volts. The norm is therefore not an instantaneous amplitude or physical energy without an appropriate interpretation.

## Inner products, orthogonality and Hilbert spaces {#S7}

For ordinary vectors, the scalar product was a sum of products of components. For real-valued functions, it can be replaced by an integral:

$$
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^b f(x)\,g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The inner product of f and g is the integral from a to b of the product of their function values.’ For complex-valued functions, the usual definition takes the **complex conjugate of one of the factors**; this distinction is important (M2-L5).

If the inner product is zero, the functions are called **orthogonal**. Thus, even two completely different signal histories can be perpendicular to each other in this mathematical sense. This is the basis of Fourier series and many filtering and approximation methods.

**Check example for orthogonality:** On $[0,1]$, let $f(x)=1$ and $g(x)=2x-1$. Then $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1(2x-1)\,\mathrm{d}x={[x^{2}-x]}_0^1=0$. Both functions are non-zero and yet orthogonal. For complex-valued functions, either the first or the second factor is conjugated, depending on the convention; the chosen convention must be established consistently.

A **Hilbert space** is a space with an inner product that is **complete** with respect to the resulting norm. Roughly speaking, completeness means that if the elements of a sequence get closer and closer together according to the appropriate distance rule (a *Cauchy sequence*), the sequence has a limit **within the same space**. The space $L^{2}$ is an important example.

The distinction from a **Banach space** is also important: completeness with respect to a norm is sufficient there, and that norm need not arise from an inner product. For example, $L^{1}$ is a Banach space but, with its usual norm, is not generally a Hilbert space.

## Bridge to probability {#S8}

Probability theory uses a set of possible outcomes, a family of measurable events and a measure whose total value is one. We often denote this **probability measure** by $P$.

A random variable is a suitable measurable function on the sample space. Its expected value can be understood as an integral **with respect to this probability measure**:

$$
E[X]=\int X\,\mathrm{d}P\qquad\text{if }\int\lvert X\rvert\,\mathrm{d}P<\infty
$$

**Spoken:** ‘The expected value of X is the integral of X with respect to P.’ For a **finite real-valued** expectation, this representation requires, in particular, $\int\lvert X\rvert\,\mathrm{d}P<\infty$; otherwise, the expression may be undefined or take an extended value. This provides a common language for discrete sums and continuous expected values. It makes clear why statistical models and general integration are closely related.

## What is it useful for? {#S9}

- **Signal processing:** Comparing signals as elements of a function space, calculating energy and error norms and forming orthogonal decompositions (M2-L9).
- **AI and optimisation:** Measuring errors over an entire data or time interval as a norm; considering linear operators on functions rather than only on finite-dimensional vectors.
- **Differential equations:** Analysing approximate solutions, even when classical derivatives do not exist everywhere (M2-L8).
- **Probability:** Describing expected values and convergence of random variables precisely (M1-L7).

## Assumptions, limitations and common misconceptions {#S10}

- Not every subset of an arbitrary underlying set is automatically measurable; the concept of measure requires a suitable domain.
- A set of measure zero is not necessarily empty.
- For $L^{2}$, the **size measured by integrating squared magnitudes** is what matters; a function may differ at an individual point and still represent the same $L^{2}$ element.
- ‘Almost everywhere’ means ‘outside a set of measure zero’, not ‘in quite a large number of examples’.
- Convergence of functions depends on **which kind of convergence** is meant: pointwise convergence, uniform convergence, convergence in a norm and almost-everywhere convergence are different statements.
- The $L^{2}$ norm and physical energy are often related, but the units, domain of integration and interpretation of the model must be defined appropriately.
- Membership of $L^{1}$ or $L^{2}$ depends on the measure space. On an interval of finite length, $L^{2}$ is contained in $L^{1}$; this general inclusion does not hold on infinite measure spaces.
