# M2-L3 – Abstract algebra: groups, rings, fields and symmetries {#L3}

**Course area:** M2 – Advanced Bachelor’s studies / transition to Master’s studies

**Level:** Bachelor’s / bridge to Master’s studies

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** University – further advanced studies

## What is this about? {#S1}

So far, we have performed calculations with numbers, vectors and matrices. Abstract algebra asks a different question: **What rules must operations satisfy so that we can predict their results in general?** The objects need not be ordinary numbers. Rotations of a solid, permutations of elements and changes of state in a program can also form algebraic structures. The most important basic concepts are **group**, **ring** and **field**. They describe different sets of rules for operations.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine a clock with five positions. If you move four steps forward from position three, you wrap around and arrive at position two. Similarly, rotations of a square can be performed one after another. The primary question is not *what* the elements are made of, but how the operations on them work.

In simple terms, an algebraic structure consists of a **set of elements** and one or more **operations** on those elements. The operations must obey certain rules. This makes it possible to prove theorems that hold for all suitable structures – regardless of whether they consist of numbers, rotations or software states.

## New symbols before the first formula {#S3}

The set $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers, as well as $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ (complex numbers, covered in detail in M2-L5), are number systems from earlier chapters. $\mathbb{Z}_5$ does **not** denote the integers from zero to five inclusive, but exactly five residue classes: zero, one, two, three and four.

## Group: an operation with reliable rules {#S4}

A **group** is a non-empty set $G$ equipped with an operation $\circ$. The following four requirements must be satisfied:

1.  **Closure:** Combining two elements of $G$ produces another element of $G$.
2.  **Associativity:** For three elements, the placement of brackets may change without affecting the result.
3.  **Identity element:** There is an element $e$ that leaves an element unchanged when combined with it.
4.  **Inverse elements:** For every element, there is a corresponding inverse that produces $e$ when combined with it.

The operation must genuinely assign two elements of $G$ **to another element of $G$** (a closed binary operation). The identity element acts as an identity **on both sides**; the inverse must also work on both sides. For example, the integers under addition satisfy these rules; the natural numbers, without negative numbers, do not have additive inverses for positive elements.

The two central statements about the operation are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a\circ b)\circ c&=a\circ(b\circ c)\\
a\circ a^{-1}&=a^{-1}\circ a=e
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud, first line:** ‘Combining a with b and then combining the result with c is equivalent to combining b with c first and then combining a with that result.’ **Second line:** ‘a combined with its inverse element gives the identity element; here, this also holds in the reverse order.’

In each case, the assumption is that $a$, $b$ and $c$ belong to the same group $G$. Groups do **not** require $a\circ b$ to give the same result as $b\circ a$. If commutativity also holds, the group is called *abelian* or *commutative*.

## Complete mini-example: arithmetic modulo five {#S5}

Consider the five possible remainders after division by five. We use **addition modulo five** as the operation: first add, then retain only the remainder after division by five.

$$
\begin{aligned}
3+4&=7\\
7\bmod 5&=2\\
\text{therefore: }\quad 3+4&\equiv2\pmod{5}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Three plus four is seven; seven leaves a remainder of two when divided by five. Therefore, three plus four and two are congruent modulo five.’

Addition modulo five always remains within the five residue classes. It is associative, zero is the identity element, and every residue class has an additive inverse. For example, the additive inverse of residue class three is residue class two, since $3+2\equiv0\pmod5$.

| Residue class | Additive inverse modulo 5 |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 4 |
| 2 | 3 |
| 3 | 2 |
| 4 | 1 |

The five residue classes with this addition therefore form an **abelian group**. The example is more than a numerical trick: it models cyclic counters, circular buffers and recurring state transitions.

**Typical error:** The fact that $\mathbb{Z}_5$ is an additive group does not imply that any ordinary division is possible. Division requires additional conditions on *multiplication*.

## Symmetries as groups {#S6}

When we rotate a square about its centre, its shape remains unchanged under rotations of 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. Performing these four rotations in succession corresponds to adding their angles modulo 360°. This **rotation group** has four elements and is abelian.

If we also include reflections, we obtain the eight symmetries of a square. This group is often denoted by $D_4$ (the numbering of dihedral groups varies between textbooks). Here, **the order can matter**: reflecting first and then rotating generally gives a different transformation from rotating first and then reflecting.

### Countercheck: the order of two symmetries {#abschnitt-7}

Let $R$ denote the rotation of the square by 90° anticlockwise and $S$ the reflection in the horizontal axis. At the point $(1,0)$, we can see the difference explicitly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{first S, then R:}\quad(1,0)\to(1,0)\to(0,1)\\
&\text{first R, then S:}\quad(1,0)\to(0,1)\to(0,-1)
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘With reflection followed by rotation, the point ends up above; in the reverse order, it ends up below.’ These two symmetries therefore do not commute. This is concrete evidence of the **non-commutativity** of the full symmetry group of the square. $R$ and $S$ are names for transformations here, not new numbers.

This reveals an important insight: **rules for operations can work without ordinary numbers.** The symmetry group is useful in crystallography, computer graphics, pattern recognition and the identification of equivalent configurations, among other areas.

## Rings: two compatible operations {#S7}

A **ring** has two operations, usually written as addition and multiplication. Addition forms an abelian group. Multiplication is associative and is compatible with addition through the **distributive law**.

$$
\begin{aligned}
a(b+c)&=ab+ac\\
(a+b)c&=ac+bc
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘a times the sum of b and c is a times b plus a times c’, and ‘the sum of a and b, multiplied by c, is a times c plus b times c’.

The integers $\mathbb{Z}$, with their usual addition and multiplication, form a **commutative ring with identity**. The term ‘with identity’ explicitly indicates that there is a multiplicative identity element; some general definitions of a ring do not require one. Ordinary multiplication of integers is commutative, but this is **not true of every ring**: for instance, multiplication of square matrices is generally not commutative.

## Fields: division by any non-zero element {#S8}

A **field** is (in simple terms) a commutative ring with identity in which every non-zero number has a multiplicative inverse. This makes division by any non-zero element possible.

Examples are $\mathbb{Q}$ (rational numbers), $\mathbb{R}$ (real numbers) and $\mathbb{C}$ (complex numbers). The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ do **not form a field**, because the number two has no multiplicative reciprocal within the integers.

For a positive modulus $n$, the residue classes with addition and multiplication modulo $n$ always form a commutative ring with identity. For $n\geq2$, a **field** is obtained precisely when $n$ is prime. In particular, $\mathbb{Z}_5$, with addition and multiplication modulo five, is a field because five is prime. For example:

$$
2\cdot3=6\equiv1\pmod5
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Two times three is six, which has the same remainder as one modulo five.’ Residue class three is therefore the multiplicative inverse of residue class two. By comparison, residue classes modulo six with ordinary modular multiplication do **not form a field**, because $2\cdot3\equiv0\pmod6$ even though neither factor is zero. This shows why the modulus must be chosen carefully in finite residue-class systems. In $\mathbb{Z}_6$, residue class two cannot be turned into one by multiplying it by another residue class. An equation such as $2x\equiv2\pmod6$ even has several solutions: $x\equiv1$ and $x\equiv4$ both work. Anyone who ‘divides by two’ without considering this may therefore incorrectly claim uniqueness.

**A precise structural note:** A field additionally requires its additive zero and multiplicative identity to be distinct ($0\neq1$). The residue classes modulo one therefore do not form a field.

| Structure | Minimum requirements | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Group | One associative operation, identity element, inverses | Rotations of a square; addition modulo 5 |
| Ring | Additive abelian group, associative multiplication, distributivity | Integers, square matrices |
| Field | Commutative ring with 1, all non-zero elements multiplicatively invertible | Rational/real/complex numbers; residue classes modulo a prime |

## Preserving structures: homomorphisms {#S9}

A **homomorphism** translates one algebraic structure into another while respecting its operation. For instance, integers can be mapped to their residue classes modulo five: adding before or after taking the remainders leads to the same residue class.

To check this, take $7+9=16$: both the remainder of 16 and the sum of the remainders of seven and nine give residue class one modulo five. In general, this holds for any integers. However, the mapping **loses information**: two and seven give the same residue class even though they are different integers. It therefore cannot be reversed uniquely in a one-to-one manner.

Abstract algebra studies which properties are preserved in this process. If such a structure-preserving mapping is **bijective** (every target element is reached by exactly one source element), it is called an **isomorphism**. Two isomorphic structures therefore differ in their naming, but not in their essential operational rules.

The precise formal framework is a topic for advanced university studies. For our reading course, the guiding question is initially enough: *Which properties remain the same when we represent a mathematical system differently?*

## What is this useful for? {#S10}

- **Cryptography and coding:** Finite fields and residue classes play a role in many cryptographic and error-correcting methods. However, mathematical structure alone does not make a method secure.
- **Software and algorithms:** Cyclic counters, checksums and data structures use modular arithmetic; algebraic laws help combine operations correctly.
- **3D graphics and robotics:** Rotations and symmetries can be compared structurally. Three-dimensional rotations form a non-commutative group under composition.
- **Mathematical modelling:** A known statement can be transferred to new classes of objects as soon as the same axioms are satisfied.

## Assumptions, limitations and typical misconceptions {#S11}

- The word ‘group’ does not simply mean ‘any collection’; the operational rules are decisive.
- A group has **one** selected group operation; rings and fields require several mutually compatible operations.
- Not every group is commutative. The order of spatial transformations is often decisive.
- Modular arithmetic is not automatically ordinary arithmetic: division and inverses depend on the number system.
- Correct algebraic equations alone are not sufficient for cryptography; specific security assumptions, protocols and implementations must be examined separately.
