# M1-L3 – University: Analysis II – Continuity, differentiation and integration {#L3}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor’s: mathematical foundations

**Level:** Bachelor

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Core university subject · Functions of one real variable

## What is this about? {#S1}

In upper secondary school, we learnt how to calculate function values, slopes and integrals. At university, a further question arises: **Under what conditions is a calculation justified in the first place?** A curve may have a break or a corner at a particular point, or behave so irregularly that familiar methods fail. Mathematical analysis investigates these limiting cases using precise definitions. In this lesson, we initially restrict ourselves to functions that assign exactly one real value to each real input.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

A function is **continuous** if a sufficiently small change in the input allows the change in the result to be made arbitrarily small, with respect to a fixed point under investigation. It is **differentiable** if a well-defined local linear approximation exists: the graph has a unique finite tangent slope there. In our first notion of integration, **integrable** means that a sequence of increasingly fine sums determines a common value. These three properties are related, but they are not identical.

## New symbols – read first {#S3}

| Symbol | Name / pronunciation | Meaning here | Other uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_0$ | “x nought” | Fixed point at which a function is investigated | Initial value or measurement index |
| $\delta$ | “delta”, lower-case Greek delta | Permitted distance between the input and the point under investigation | General parameter or variation |
| $\varepsilon$ | “epsilon” | Specified positive tolerance for the function value; familiar from M1-L2 | Error bound in numerical methods |
| `∀` / `∃` | “for all” / “there exists” | Precise statement about arbitrary tolerances and suitable bounds | Logic in general |
| $\lvert x-x_0\rvert$ | “absolute value of x minus x nought” | Distance on the number line | Modulus of complex numbers |
| $\lim_{x\to x_0}$ | “limit as x approaches x nought” | Limit when approaching a point | Limit of a sequence, see M1-L2 |
| $h$ | “aitch” | Small, non-zero input increment | Height, step size in numerical methods |
| $f^{\prime}(x_0)$ | “f prime at x nought” | Derivative of f at the point under consideration | A prime may otherwise distinguish a different object |
| `Σ` | “capital sigma” | Sum of many individual contributions | Summation symbol in statistics and series |
| $\Delta x$ | “delta x” | Width of a subinterval | General change in a value |
| $\int_a^b$ | “integral from a to b” | Accumulation over the interval from a to b | Multidimensional integrals later |
| $\mathrm{d}x$ | “d x” | Variable of integration and formal differential symbol | Different notational context in differential calculus |
| $F^{\prime}=f$ | “capital F prime equals lower-case f” | F is an antiderivative of f | Relationship between two functions in general |

The letters $a$ and $b$ denote the **lower and upper limits** of the integral. $f$ is a function; $F$ here denotes one of its antiderivatives. The square-bracket interval notation $[a,b]$ means that both endpoints are included. A colon after a logical condition is read as “such that”.

## Continuity: no break at the point under consideration {#S4}

The precise question is: can I guarantee **any desired tolerance for the result, however small**, by choosing a suitably small tolerance for the input?

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\\
&\qquad |x-x_0|<\delta\implies |f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read in full:** “For every positive epsilon there exists a positive delta such that, for all permitted inputs x whose distance from x nought is less than delta, the distance from f of x to f of x nought is also less than epsilon.” The condition “for all x” applies **only to admissible x in the domain**; at boundary points, it may be possible to approach only from the side available there. The point $x_0$ itself must belong to the domain. The chosen input tolerance $\delta$ may depend on $\varepsilon$ and on the point $x_0$ under investigation. Importantly, we are testing continuity **at a fixed point**, not the stronger property of uniform continuity over an entire region. The abbreviated formula must be read as a statement about every $x$ in the domain of the function.

**Mini-example – the square function at two.** We consider $f(x)=x^2$ and $x_0=2$. The relationship between the deviations in input and result is:

$$
\lvert f(x)-f(2)\rvert=\lvert x^2-4\rvert=\lvert x-2\rvert\cdot\lvert x+2\rvert
$$

If the input is less than $1$ away from $2$, then $1<x<3$ and hence $\lvert x+2\rvert<5$. For any desired tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ in the result, it therefore suffices to choose $\delta=\min(1,\varepsilon/5)$: from $\lvert x-2\rvert<\delta$ it follows that $\lvert x^2-4\rvert<5\cdot\lvert x-2\rvert<5\delta\le\varepsilon$. The final symbol “≤” means “less than or equal to”. The crucial point is the **strict** bound in the initial condition $\lvert x-2\rvert<\delta$; even when $5\delta=\varepsilon$, the result remains strictly less than $\varepsilon$. $\min$ means “the smaller of the specified values”. This essentially shows **why** the square function is continuous at this point, rather than merely looking at its graph.

Continuity requires the point under consideration to belong to the domain. The quotient $1/x$ is undefined at $x=0$. Therefore, for the natural domain $\mathbb{R}$ excluding zero, “continuity at zero” is **undefined**, rather than simply “false”. On an extended domain with a specially assigned value at zero, the formula $1/x$ could not be extended continuously to that point, since the values become unbounded as we approach it. At every other real point, $1/x$ is continuous.

## Differentiability: a unique local slope {#S5}

In school mathematics M2-L3 (German-language course), the derivative was introduced as the limit of secant slopes. We now consider it as a question of existence:

$$
f^{\prime}(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}
$$

**Read in full:** “The derivative of f at x nought is the limit of the quotient of the change in the function value and the input increment h, as h approaches zero.” While division takes place, $h$ must be non-zero. At an interior point of the domain, the ordinary derivative exists only if the limits from both sides agree and are finite. At an endpoint of an interval, a **one-sided derivative** may be defined instead; it is not automatically a two-sided derivative.

**Mini-example – the square function again.** At $x_0=2$, we have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}{h}
&=\frac{{(2+h)}^2-4}{h}\\
&=\frac{4h+h^2}{h}\\
&=4+h\quad\text{(for }h\ne0\text{)}\\
\text{Limit as }h\to0&:\quad 4
\end{aligned}
$$

The slope is therefore $f^{\prime}(2)=4$. Locally, the graph can be approximated by a straight line with slope four. The method does not use an “infinitesimal number” as an ordinary divisor, but instead takes a limit.

**An important theorem:** Differentiability at a point implies continuity there. The converse is not true. The absolute value function $f(x)=\lvert x\rvert$ is continuous at zero, but has a corner: the slope from the left is $-1$, and from the right $+1$. Since there is no common finite derivative, it is not differentiable there.

## Integration: from a collection of rectangles to a limit {#S6}

For suitable functions, a definite integral can be understood as a limit of Riemann sums. We divide a finite interval into small subintervals, choose a sample point in each one, multiply its function value by the width of the subinterval, and add all the products. If **every sequence of sufficiently fine partitions** in the usual Riemann sense gives the same limit, the function is integrable.

For an equal subdivision and right-hand sample points, one possible sum is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta x&=\frac{b-a}{n}\\
S_n&=\sum_{k=1}^n f(a+k\cdot\Delta x)\cdot\Delta x\\
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x&=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_n
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** “Delta x is the length of the interval divided by the number of subintervals. S sub n is the sum of the function height at each right endpoint multiplied by the subinterval width. The definite integral is the limit of these sums.” Here, $n$ is the number of subintervals and $k$ is their running index. The final equation holds for Riemann-integrable functions; it does not define a number that always exists.

**Mini-example – the straight line on the unit interval.** For $f(x)=x$, $a=0$ and $b=1$, each width is $1/n$. Using right-hand sample points, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_n&=\frac{1}{n^2}\cdot(1+2+\ldots+n)\\
&=\frac{n+1}{2n}\\
&\quad\text{As }n\to\infty\text{, }S_n\text{ approaches }\tfrac12\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

This value equals the area of the right-angled triangle beneath the straight line from zero to one. **Verification for arbitrary sample points:** the left rectangle sums are $L_n=\frac{n-1}{2n}$, and the right ones are $S_n=\frac{n+1}{2n}$. Since $f(x)=x$ is monotonically increasing, every admissible sample-point sum for each of these equal partitions lies between $L_n$ and $S_n$. Their difference $S_n-L_n=\frac1n$ tends to zero; both limits are $\frac12$. We thus see more than just the convergence of one specially chosen right-hand sum. For full Riemann integrability, the corresponding partition condition is decisive, not merely a successful sequence of approximations. For functions with negative values, however, the integral gives a **signed balance**, not automatically a positive area (see school mathematics M2-L8, German-language course).

Every real-valued function that is **continuous on a closed, bounded interval** is Riemann-integrable there. The converse is not true: a function with finitely many jump discontinuities may also be integrable. Highly discontinuous functions require careful distinctions; measure theory later introduces a more general notion of integration.

## The fundamental theorem – why differentiation and integration fit together {#S7}

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ there, then:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=F(b)-F(a)
$$

**Read in full:** “The definite integral of f of x from a to b is the antiderivative at the upper limit minus the antiderivative at the lower limit.” This equation is a statement with prerequisites, not merely a computational trick. **The other direction of the fundamental theorem:** if, for a function $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$, we define the accumulation function $G(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t$, then $G^{\prime}(x)=f(x)$ at every interior point; suitable one-sided statements apply at the endpoints. Here, $t$ is simply the running variable of integration. For example, $f(t)=2t$ with $a=0$ gives the function $G(x)=x^2$, whose derivative is $2x$. The fundamental theorem thus connects both directions: local differentiation and accumulation over an interval.

## What is it useful for? {#S8}

In **numerical methods**, continuity explains why small changes in the input cause small changes in the result under suitable conditions; this helps in evaluating computational models. Differentiability is important for automatic differentiation, optimisation algorithms and local sensitivity analyses. Integrals determine, for example, the total amount of data or energy accumulated from a continuous rate. In control engineering and robotics, the clear distinction between an ideal limit and finite measurement resolution is particularly important.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S9}

- Continuity **at a point** does not automatically mean uniform continuity throughout the domain. A closed, bounded interval provides further theorems.
- Differentiability is stronger than continuity; a corner or cusp may prevent a derivative from existing.
- A definite integral can be negative; “area” and “balance” are not interchangeable.
- Numerical differences with a very small step size can become unstable due to rounding errors. A Riemann sum for finite $n$ is only an approximation.
- The Riemann notion of integration explained here is not the only mathematical notion of integration. Measure theory will be discussed later in greater depth.
