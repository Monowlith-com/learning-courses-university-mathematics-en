# M3-L2 – Master: Operator theory and spectral theory {#L2}

**Course area:** M3 – Master’s-oriented advanced studies

**Level:** Master’s orientation

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Master’s-oriented advanced study following M1-L5, M1-L6, M2-L7 and M3-L1

## What is this about? {#S1}

A matrix transforms a finite number of numbers into a finite number of other numbers. An **operator**, by contrast, can transform entire functions into new functions. This allows us to study filters, integral representations and differential equations mathematically. In infinite-dimensional spaces, phenomena arise that do not occur with an ordinary matrix: an operator may have spectral values that are not eigenvalues with genuine eigenfunctions. M3-L2 therefore brings together the concepts of a **Hilbert space, bounded operator, adjoint operator and spectrum**.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Imagine an audio file not as a fixed list of samples, but as an ideal square-integrable function. A filter is then a mapping from functions to functions. Some filters preserve constant signal components, suppress others or assign a different weight to each instant. To establish mathematical bounds on such procedures rather than merely trying them out, we ask about their norm, linearity, invertibility and spectral behaviour.

## New notation – read this first {#S3}

| Symbol | Read aloud | Meaning here | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $H$ | ‘capital H’ | Hilbert space of the functions under consideration | H in M2-L10 denotes entropy |
| $L^{2}([0,1])$ | ‘L two on zero to one’ | Space of square-integrable functions, equal almost everywhere | Not a set of individual samples |
| $\langle f,g\rangle$ | ‘inner product of f and g’ | Similarity/overlap between two elements | Not necessarily a product at a single point |
| $\Vert f\Vert$ | ‘norm of f’ | Size of an element in the space | Absolute value of an individual number |
| $T:H\to H$ | ‘operator T from H to H’ | Mapping of entire elements of the space | Matrix representation only with a chosen basis |
| $\Vert T\Vert$ | ‘operator norm of T’ | Greatest possible relative amplification under T | Norm of an individual vector |
| $T^*$ | ‘T adjoint’ | Operator that changes the side on which T acts in the inner product | Complex conjugation alone |
| $I$ | ‘identity’ | Operator that leaves elements unchanged | Different meaning from an interval |
| $\lambda$ | ‘lambda’ | Complex parameter of the spectrum | An eigenvalue only in a special case |
| $\sigma (T)$ | ‘sigma of T’ | Spectrum of the operator | Standard deviation $\sigma$ |
| $\ker (T)$ | ‘kernel of T’ | All inputs that T maps to zero | Not a hardware kernel |
| $P^{2}=P$ | ‘P squared equals P’ | A projection produces no further change when applied repeatedly | P as a probability |

A **Hilbert space** is a vector space with an inner product whose induced norm is complete. Complete means here that every Cauchy sequence with respect to this norm has a limit **in the same space**. In the complex case, the inner product is sesquilinear and involves conjugation; in the real case, ordinary symmetry suffices.

## The function space $L^2$ as our setting {#S4}

For real-valued square-integrable functions on $[0,1]$, we choose:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle f,g\rangle&=\int_0^1 f(x)g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\lVert f\rVert_2&=\sqrt{\int_0^1|f(x)|^2\,\mathrm{d}x}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: ‘The inner product is the integral of the product of the two functions; the L-two norm is the square root of the integral of the squared absolute value.’ For complex functions, one of the two functions is conjugated in the inner product; which one depends on the stated convention. The space contains **equivalence classes** of functions that are equal almost everywhere, not pointwise formulae considered independently of one another.

**Worked example:** For $f(x)=x$, the squared norm is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert f\rVert_2^2&=\int_0^1x^2\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac13\\
\lVert f\rVert_2&=\frac1{\sqrt3}
\end{aligned}
$$

A small $L^2$ norm does not guarantee that a function is small *at every point*. A high peak on an extremely narrow interval may have a small total squared error.

## Boundedness of a linear operator {#S5}

An operator T is **linear** if $T(af+bg)=aT(f)+bT(g)$ for admissible scalars a,b and functions f,g. It is **bounded** if there exists a finite number C such that:

$$
\lVert T(f)\rVert\leq C\lVert f\rVert\qquad\text{for all }f\in H
$$

Read aloud: ‘The size of the result is at most C times the size of the input.’ The smallest possible bound is the operator norm $\Vert T\Vert$. For the bounded operator considered here, it is also the supremum of $\Vert Tf\Vert$ over all inputs with $\Vert f\Vert =1$. For linear operators defined on the entire normed space, boundedness is equivalent to continuity. The differentiation operator, for example, cannot simply be treated as an everywhere-defined bounded operator on $L^2$; its domain is therefore no minor detail.

## A fully worked example of a projection operator {#S6}

On the real space $H=L^{2}([0,1])$, we define the operator P by:

$$
(Pf)(x)=\left(\int_0^1 f(t)\,\mathrm{d}t\right)\cdot1
$$

Read aloud: ‘P replaces a function with the constant function whose value is the mean of the original signal over the unit interval.’ The number resulting from the integral no longer depends on x. The result is therefore always constant.

**Specific signal:** Let $f(x)=2x$. The mean is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 2t\,\mathrm{d}t&=\left[t^2\right]_0^1=1\\
(Pf)(x)&=1
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying P a second time leaves this constant function unchanged:

$$
P(Pf)=Pf\qquad\text{so }P^2=P
$$

P is therefore called a **projection**. The inner product with another function g also shows that P is self-adjoint:

$$
\langle Pf,g\rangle=\left(\int_0^1 f\right)\left(\int_0^1 g\right)=\langle f,Pg\rangle
$$

Read aloud: ‘It makes no difference whether we project f first and then compare it with g, or project g first and then compare it with f.’ This holds here for the stated real functions; it also holds on the complex Hilbert space with the correct conjugation convention.

For P, $\Vert Pf\Vert _{2}\le \Vert f\Vert _{2}$ follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on $[0,1]$. Equality is attained by any non-zero constant function. Therefore $\Vert P\Vert =1$.

## Eigenfunctions and spectrum {#S7}

For a matrix, we learnt that $Av=\lambda v$ means that the vector v is merely scaled by A. For operators, the same idea is expressed as:

$$
T(f)=\lambda f\qquad\text{with }f\neq0
$$

Read aloud: ‘A non-zero function f is simply multiplied by the number lambda by the operator T.’ Then f is an **eigenfunction**, and $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue.

For the projection operator P, we immediately find two types:

- Every non-zero **constant** function is mapped to itself: eigenvalue $\lambda =1$.
- Every non-zero function with **zero mean** is mapped to the zero function: eigenvalue $\lambda =0$; example $g(x)=x-\frac12$.

For this operator, the complete spectrum is $\sigma (P)=\{0,1\}$. We can justify this because every vector can be decomposed into a constant part and a zero-mean part. For any other $\lambda$, $P-\lambda I$ is invertible with a bounded inverse operator. **Convention:** For general spectral statements about operators on real Hilbert spaces, one usually considers the **complexification** and tests invertibility for complex $\lambda$. The examples used here have precisely the stated spectra under this approach as well.

**Why is the spectrum more than a list of eigenvalues?** Consider the multiplication operator $M$ on $L^{2}([0,1])$:

$$
(Mf)(x)=x f(x)
$$

It multiplies the signal value at each point by x. Its norm is one and it is self-adjoint. Its spectrum is the **entire interval $[0,1]$**. Nevertheless, this operator has **no eigenvalues**: $x f(x)=\lambda  f(x)$ means that f would have to be zero except at the single point x=$\lambda$. However, a function that is non-zero at only one point represents the zero vector in $L^2$. The distinction between a spectral value and an eigenvalue is typical of infinite-dimensional spaces.

### Proof without an eigenfunction: why a spectral value still arises {#abschnitt-8}

The multiplication operator $M$ from section 4 provides a concrete example of this distinction. For a complex parameter $\lambda$ **outside** the interval $[0,1]$, the equation $(M-\lambda I)f=g$ can be solved by

$$
f(x)=\frac{g(x)}{x-\lambda}\text{.}
$$

The denominator stays away from zero there. If we set $d=\inf \{|x-\lambda | : 0\le x\le 1\}>0$, then $\Vert f\Vert_2\le\frac{\Vert g\Vert_2}{d}$. The inverse operator is therefore bounded: **such $\lambda$ are not in the spectrum.** Here, ‘infimum’ means the smallest distance attainable in the limiting sense.

For a value $\lambda$ **in the interior** $(0,1)$, by contrast, we consider functions whose support is narrow and centred on $\lambda$. Choose $\varepsilon >0$ so small that $(\lambda -\varepsilon ,\lambda +\varepsilon )$ lies within the unit interval, and set

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_\varepsilon(x)&=\begin{cases}
\frac1{\sqrt{2\varepsilon}}&\text{for }|x-\lambda|<\varepsilon,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}\\
\lVert f_\varepsilon\rVert_2^2&=(2\varepsilon)\cdot\frac1{2\varepsilon}=1\\
\lVert(M-\lambda I)f_\varepsilon\rVert_2&\le\varepsilon\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘These normalised functions always have the same size in the L-two sense; after applying M minus lambda, however, their images become arbitrarily small.’ If ${(M-\lambda I)}^{-1}$ were an everywhere-defined **bounded** inverse operator, there would be a fixed lower bound $\Vert (M-\lambda I)f\Vert _{2}\ge c\Vert f\Vert _{2}$ with $c>0$; the example functions contradict this for $\varepsilon \to 0$. The endpoints 0 and 1 also belong to the spectrum by considering narrow one-sided intervals.

**Important distinction:** None of these functions is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $\lambda$. An actual eigenvector would satisfy $(M-\lambda I)f=0$. Here, only the **norms of the images** approach zero. This is a verifiable form of *approximate spectral behaviour*, not a claim about a non-existent eigenvector.

## Adjoint, self-adjoint and compact operators {#S8}

The **adjoint** $T^*$ of a bounded operator is defined by an inner product equation. Under the real convention chosen here:

$$
\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle f,T^*g\rangle
$$

Read aloud: ‘T can be moved to the other side of the inner product if it is replaced by its adjoint.’ If $T^*=T$, we call T **self-adjoint**. In complex Hilbert spaces, this property is particularly valuable because the spectral values are then real.

A **compact operator** maps bounded sequences to sequences whose images have a convergent subsequence. The projection operator P is compact because its range is only one-dimensional. The multiplication operator M above on $L^2$, on the other hand, is not compact. Compactness explains why some infinite-dimensional operators can be studied in much the same way as matrices in essential respects.

## From spectra to applied mathematics {#S9}

Spectra help us study differential equations, image and signal filters, and questions of stability. In numerical mathematics, operators are approximated by matrices; this is useful, but an eigenvalue of a discretised matrix is **not automatically** an exact eigenvalue of the continuous operator. We need results about the convergence of the discretisation and its spectral reliability.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

- **Signal processing:** Projections separate signal components, for example constant means from fluctuations.
- **PDEs:** Differential and integral operators govern propagation, stability and boundary-value problems.
- **AI/numerical mathematics:** Low-rank approximations, covariance operators and kernel methods are shaped by the concept of an operator.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

This lesson works with the **real** $L^2$ space unless the complex extension is explicitly mentioned. Some general spectral theorems require complex Hilbert spaces, self-adjointness or other conditions on operators. Unbounded differential operators require carefully specified domains. A matrix approximation is no substitute for a proof about the original operator.
