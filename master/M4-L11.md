## What is this about? {#S1}

A robot does not know its position simply because it receives a single GPS reading, camera observation or distance measurement. Every sensor may be noisy, unavailable, delayed or systematically inaccurate. At the same time, the robot changes between two measurements. **Probabilistic robotics** therefore combines previous estimates, motion models and new observations in a mathematical procedure. For control, the result provides not only a position but also **an indication of the remaining uncertainty**.

## Basic idea: predict, observe, correct {#S2}

Imagine a mobile robot located either on the left or on the right of a corridor. Its motor commands suggest a movement, but the wheels may slip. A camera detects a red wall but may misinterpret colours. The position we seek is therefore not simply “the latest measurement”. Instead, we calculate it in two steps:

1.  **Prediction:** Which position is likely according to the motion model?
2.  **Measurement correction:** How likely would the observed sensor information be at each of these positions?

This repeated procedure is called a **Bayes filter**. It generalises Bayes' formula from M1-L7 to state estimation over time.

The superscript `⁻` in $\operatorname{bel}_{t}^{-}$ means **prediction before the current measurement**, not an inverse or negative probability. In the covariance matrix $P$, the variances of the state components are on the diagonal. The off-diagonal entries are the covariances between the state components.

## The Bayes filter in two mathematical steps {#S4}

For a **discrete** state space, the prediction is obtained as follows:

$$
\operatorname{bel}_t^{-}(x_t)=\sum_{x_{t-1}}p(x_t\mid x_{t-1},u_t)\cdot\operatorname{bel}_{t-1}(x_{t-1})
$$

**In words:** We consider all possible previous states. For each one, we multiply its previous probability by the probability of reaching the new state through the control command. We then add these contributions. For continuous state spaces, an integral replaces the sum.

After the measurement, we proceed **under the usual model assumption** that, given the current state, the current measurement requires no additional information from previous measurements or control inputs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{bel}_t(x_t)&\propto p(z_t\mid x_t)\cdot\operatorname{bel}_t^-(x_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

**In words:** The new state probability is proportional to the probability of observing the actual measurement in that state, multiplied by the predicted probability of the state. We then have to **normalise** over all states so that their probabilities add up to one. The conditional measurement probability $p(z_{t} | x_{t})$ is the **likelihood**, not already the final posterior.

### Fully worked example: left or right? {#abschnitt-5}

Before the movement, the robot is on the left with probability $0{,}6$ and on the right with probability $0{,}4$. The control command has the following modelled transitions:

| Starting position | To the left | To the right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Left | 0,8 | 0,2 |
| Right | 0,3 | 0,7 |

Each row adds up to one. First, we calculate the state prediction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{new left})&=0{,}6\cdot0{,}8+0{,}4\cdot0{,}3=0{,}48+0{,}12=0{,}60\\
P(\text{new right})&=0{,}6\cdot0{,}2+0{,}4\cdot0{,}7=0{,}12+0{,}28=0{,}40
\end{aligned}
$$

The camera now reports the feature “red wall”. Our simplified measurement model states that if the robot is on the **left**, this signal occurs with probability $0{,}9$; if it is on the **right**, with probability $0{,}2$. The **unnormalised** weights are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Left: }\quad&0{,}9\cdot0{,}60=0{,}54\\
\text{Right: }\quad&0{,}2\cdot0{,}40=0{,}08\\
\text{Normalisation: }\quad&0{,}54+0{,}08=0{,}62
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing both weights by 0,62 gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{left}\mid\text{red wall})&=\frac{0{,}54}{0{,}62}=\frac{27}{31}\approx0{,}8710\\
P(\text{right}\mid\text{red wall})&=\frac{0{,}08}{0{,}62}=\frac4{31}\approx0{,}1290\\
\text{sum}&=\frac{31}{31}=1
\end{aligned}
$$

**Interpretation:** The measurement makes “left” considerably more likely. However, it does not prove that the robot is actually on the left. The statement $87{,}1\,\%$ depends directly on the assumed transition and sensor probabilities. With a miscalibrated camera model, the confidence calculated from the posterior can be misleading.

## Kalman filter: uncertainty as variance {#S5}

For **linear dynamics and observation with Gaussian noise**, a Kalman filter is often used. In a **one-dimensional** example, the state estimate consists of a mean $\mu$ and a variance $P$. A new measurement $z$ has modelled noise variance $R$. The Kalman gain is:

$$
K=\frac{P^{-}}{P^{-}+R}
$$

**Read aloud:** “Divide the predicted state variance by the sum of the predicted state variance and the measurement variance.” The corrected estimate is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{\mathrm{neu}}&=\mu^-+K\cdot(z-\mu^-)\\
P_{\mathrm{neu}}&=(1-K)\cdot P^-
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** “Take the predicted mean and correct it by the Kalman gain multiplied by the difference between the measurement and the predicted mean.” The second equation gives the corrected variance in this simplified independent scalar case.

### Fully worked example: two measurement sources {#abschnitt-7}

A robot initially estimates its position at $10\,\mathrm m$ with standard deviation $2\,\mathrm m$. Its variance is therefore $P^-={(2\,\mathrm m)}^2=4\,\mathrm m^2$. An additional measuring device reports $z=12\,\mathrm m$ with standard deviation $1\,\mathrm m$, hence $R=1\,\mathrm m^2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
K&=\frac4{4+1}=\frac45=0{,}8\\
\text{Innovation}&=z-\mu^-=12\,\mathrm{m}-10\,\mathrm{m}=2\,\mathrm{m}\\
\mu_{\mathrm{neu}}&=10\,\mathrm{m}+0{,}8\cdot2\,\mathrm{m}=11{,}6\,\mathrm{m}\\
P_{\mathrm{neu}}&=(1-0{,}8)\cdot4\,\mathrm{m}^2=0{,}8\,\mathrm{m}^2\\
\text{new standard deviation}&=\sqrt{0{,}8}\,\mathrm{m}\approx0{,}8944\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Interpretation:** The sensor receives a high weight here because its variance is smaller. The result lies between 10 and 12 metres. The remaining uncertainty decreases in the model, but neither the noise nor a possible sensor error disappears. Most importantly, two **identical copies of the same measurement** are not independent measurements and must not be treated as though they independently reduce uncertainty twice.

### Countercheck: duplicate sensor data are not two independent measurements {#abschnitt-8}

In the scalar case shown, the Kalman gain assumes that **prediction error and measurement noise are independent**. Counting two copies of the same measurement as two independent sensors is particularly problematic. We again use $\mu^-=10\,\mathrm m$, $P^-=4\,\mathrm m^2$, $z=12\,\mathrm m$ and $R=1\,\mathrm m^2$.

When correctly included once, the measurement gives $\mu_{\mathrm{neu}}=11{,}6\,\mathrm m$ and $P_{\mathrm{neu}}=0{,}8\,\mathrm m^2$. Treating the **identical** measurement twice as independent produces an artificially smaller calculated variance:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Double counting as independent (incorrect model):}\\
P_{\mathrm{falsch}}&=\frac1{1/4+1/1+1/1}\,\mathrm{m}^2
=\frac49\,\mathrm{m}^2\approx0{,}4444\,\mathrm{m}^2\\
\mu_{\mathrm{falsch}}&=\frac{10/4+12/1+12/1}{1/4+1+1}\,\mathrm{m}\\
&=\frac{53/2}{9/4}\,\mathrm{m}=\frac{106}9\,\mathrm{m}\approx11{,}7778\,\mathrm{m}\\
&\text{Correct result for the same single piece of evidence:}\\
P_{\mathrm{korrekt}}&=\frac45\,\mathrm{m}^2=0{,}8\,\mathrm{m}^2\\
\mu_{\mathrm{korrekt}}&=\frac{58}5\,\mathrm{m}=11{,}6\,\mathrm{m}
\end{aligned}
$$

The additional apparent precision arises solely from the **incorrect independence assumption**. Two different sensors may also have correlated errors, for example because of shared preprocessing or calibration. In that case, simply weighting them using separate standard deviations is insufficient; their cross-covariance must be included in the model.

## From a single number to state vectors and covariance matrices {#S6}

In a realistic model, the state vector may contain position, orientation and velocity. Under a linear state model, the prediction equations are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_t^-&=F\cdot\mu_{t-1}+B\cdot u_t\\
P_t^-&=F\cdot P_{t-1}\cdot F^\top+Q
\end{aligned}
$$

Matrix $B$ transfers the effect of control commands to the state. **In words:** We apply the modelled motion to the previous mean and propagate the previous covariance through that motion. Process noise $Q$ is added, representing unmodelled wheel slip, for example.

For the linear observation, the Kalman gain is a matrix:

$$
K_t=P_t^{-}H^{\mathrm{T}}\left(HP_t^{-}H^{\mathrm{T}}+R\right)^{-1}
$$

The superscript `⁻¹` here denotes the **inverse of the innovation covariance**, provided that it exists. Read aloud: “Multiply the predicted covariance by the transpose of the observation matrix and then by the inverse of the total predicted measurement covariance.” In numerically stable implementations, the corresponding linear system is often solved instead of explicitly calculating a matrix inverse.

### Covariance correction and numerical stability {#abschnitt-10}

For the linear measurement step with **uncorrelated prediction error and measurement noise**, the updated covariance can also be written in the so-called *Joseph form*. It uses the same $P^-$, $K$, $H$ and $R$ as above; $I$ is the identity matrix:

$$
P^{+}=(I-KH)P^{-}{(I-KH)}^{\mathrm{T}}+KRK^{\mathrm{T}}
$$

**Read aloud:** “The remaining prediction error and the uncertainty introduced through the measurement are added as covariance contributions.” In the one-dimensional example with $H=1$, $K=\frac45$, $P^-=4\,\mathrm m^2$, $R=1\,\mathrm m^2$, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^+&=\left(1-\frac45\right)^2\cdot4\,\mathrm{m}^2+\left(\frac45\right)^2\cdot1\,\mathrm{m}^2\\
&=\frac1{25}\cdot4\,\mathrm{m}^2+\frac{16}{25}\cdot1\,\mathrm{m}^2\\
&=\frac{20}{25}\,\mathrm{m}^2=\frac45\,\mathrm{m}^2=0{,}8\,\mathrm{m}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

The Joseph form makes both sources of error visible and can help preserve symmetry and positive semidefiniteness in finite floating-point arithmetic. It does **not** remedy an incorrect assumption of independence between measurements; for correlated noise, the corresponding model assumptions must be changed.

**Scope of validity:** In the **exact classical Bayesian Kalman model**, linear state and observation equations apply, together with appropriately modelled independent, normally distributed process and measurement noise with finite covariance; the initial belief is also Gaussian. For non-Gaussian distributions, linear estimators may still be useful, but the stated means and covariances then generally do **not** describe the complete exact posterior distribution. Correlations between noise sources require additional covariance terms or an extended model. Nonlinear models lead to approximations such as the **extended Kalman filter (EKF)** or **unscented Kalman filter (UKF)**. **Particle filters** represent a belief through weighted samples and can also capture several plausible state regions. The respective limitations in accuracy and computation time differ.

## Combining coordinates correctly {#S7}

A camera measures features in its own coordinate system; a laser sensor and a robot arm often use different axes and reference points. Only after a **geometric transformation** can measurements be compared meaningfully. In the plane, for a sensor point $p_S$, the following holds:

$$
p_W=R(\varphi)p_S+t
$$

**In words:** Rotate the sensor coordinate by the angle phi, then translate it into world coordinates by the translation vector t. An example with a rotation of $90^{\text{°}}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(90^{\text{°}})&=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\\
p_S&=(1,0)\\
t&=(2,3)\\
R(90^{\text{°}})\cdot p_S&=(0,1)\\
p_W&=(0,1)+(2,3)=(2,4)
\end{aligned}
$$

The sensor point is therefore at $(2,4)$ in world coordinates. A forgotten sign or an interchanged coordinate system can make measurements appear incompatible. Three dimensions additionally involve spatial rotations and often quaternions or rigid transformations from M2-L12 and M3-L9. The **timestamps** of the measurements must also correspond: a perfectly transformed camera position from one second ago may already be outdated for a fast-moving robot.

## Sensor fusion is more than averaging {#S8}

When combining camera, LiDAR, IMU, wheel-speed sensor and GPS data, at least four things must be checked:

- **Geometry:** In which coordinate system is a measurement expressed, and are the sensors correctly calibrated extrinsically?
- **Time:** Which measurements correspond to the same state of motion; which are delayed or missing?
- **Statistics:** What are the variances, outlier rates and **correlations**? Identical or derived data must not be counted twice as independent evidence.
- **Association:** Do two features actually describe the same object? An incorrectly associated landmark can produce an apparently very precise but incorrect position.

A filter may appear numerically precise and nevertheless be inconsistent if the noise model is too optimistic. Diagnostic methods include examining innovation residuals, covariances and real test runs.

## SLAM: localising oneself and mapping the world simultaneously {#S9}

**SLAM** stands for *Simultaneous Localization and Mapping*: position and the map of the environment are estimated **jointly**. This is necessary because the map is initially unknown, while the robot's own position depends on recognising previously mapped features. The relationship sought is expressed as a joint probability distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&p(x_{0:t},m\mid z_{1:t},u_{1:t})
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** “The probability distribution of the entire state trajectory from the beginning to t and the map m, given all measurements and control inputs up to t.” The expression makes clear why map errors and position errors are **correlated**. A well-known event is **loop closure**: the robot recognises a previously visited place and can correct accumulated position errors over several sections of its journey. However, **incorrect** recognition can displace large areas of a map.

Many SLAM methods formulate the problem as **factor graph optimisation**; others use EKFs or particle filters. These methods have different requirements for memory, real-time capability, loop closure detection and modelling.

### Countercheck: without a reference, the absolute SLAM position is not unique {#abschnitt-14}

If a simple one-dimensional robot model observes only the **relative distance** $z=m-x$ between robot position $x$ and landmark $m$, a measurement $z=3\,\mathrm m$ cannot determine the absolute position. For example, both of the following states produce the same measurement:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Model A: }\quad x=2\,\mathrm m,\quad m=5\,\mathrm m
 &\implies z=m-x=3\,\mathrm m\\
\text{Model B: }\quad x=10\,\mathrm m,\quad m=13\,\mathrm m
 &\implies z=m-x=3\,\mathrm m\\
\text{In general: }\quad (x+c,m+c)&\text{ gives for every }c\text{ the same measurement.}
\end{aligned}
$$

This degree of freedom is called *gauge freedom*, or a lack of absolute anchoring. A map may be geometrically consistent internally even though its common origin has shifted. Additional information is required to establish a global reference system, such as an anchored initial position or a suitable external reference. In two or three dimensions, global rotations may also belong to these unobservable degrees of freedom. The example is explicitly a **simplified relative observation model**, not a claim that every real SLAM setup is fundamentally unidentifiable.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

Multimodal sensor fusion contributes to position estimation for delivery robots, warehouse vehicles and autonomous platforms. Combinations of camera images, inertial data and laser scans can be useful even under changing sensor conditions, provided their uncertainties are modelled honestly. For AI agents with physical effects, the belief provides a basis for **explicitly incorporating uncertainty into planning**, rather than treating a single apparently certain value as the truth.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

- Bayes filters rely on **Markov modelling**; unaccounted-for long-term influences violate this assumption.
- Classical Kalman formulae require well-defined dynamics, observations, covariances and appropriate distributional assumptions. Nonlinear and multimodal uncertainties require other methods or approximations.
- Sensors are often **dependent** in time or through shared preprocessing. Ignoring correlation leads to artificially small variance.
- A low estimated variance is **not proof of safety**. Sensor blinding, incorrect associations, environmental changes and mechanical limits must be checked separately.
- SLAM may remain ambiguous in repetitive, symmetrical or dynamic scenes; sensing alone does not solve every observability problem.
