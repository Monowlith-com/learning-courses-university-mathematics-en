# M1-L7 – University: Probability theory and mathematical statistics {#L7}

**Course area:** M1 – Bachelor’s: mathematical foundations

**Level:** Bachelor’s

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** Core area of university study · Probability theory and statistics

## What is this about? {#S1}

A mathematical model often describes a clear relationship: an input produces a specified output. With weather, sensors, network latencies or AI classifications, however, the outcome is uncertain. Probability theory describes such uncertainty through **events**, **random variables** and **distributions**. Statistics reverses the direction: from finitely many observed data points, we attempt to estimate properties of an underlying model. The essential distinction is therefore: **probability proceeds from model assumptions to possible data; statistics draws conclusions about model parameters from data.**

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

Suppose a program takes different amounts of time to process a file. Instead of predicting one exact duration, we describe possible running times and their probabilities. An expected value summarises the long-term average running time, while the variance describes fluctuations. After many actual measurements, we can estimate these unknown quantities – but must take account of the remaining uncertainty. A statistic therefore gives no certainty about an individual future program run.

## Probability space: Which outcomes are possible? {#S4}

An elementary experiment has a set $\Omega$ of all possible outcomes. An **event** is a subset of this set. A probability $P$ assigns a number between zero and one to each admissible event. The certain event has probability one; the probabilities of pairwise disjoint events can be added. More precisely, **countable additivity** applies: for a finite or countably infinite family of **pairwise disjoint** admissible events, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities. In the full university-level theory, admissible events are specified by a **sigma-algebra**; it contains the underlying set and is closed under complements and countable unions. For finite examples, the power set of all outcomes is sufficient to begin with.

**Mini-example – fair die:** $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$. Event $A=\{2,4,6\}$ denotes an even number of spots, and event $B=\{4,5,6\}$ a number greater than three. We have $P(A)=\frac36=\frac12$. The joint case $A\cap B=\{4,6\}$ has probability $\frac26=\frac13$. These values assume **a fair die**; a biased die has different weights.

## Conditional probability and independence {#S5}

When event B is already known, we consider only the cases in which B occurs. This is the idea of conditional probability:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\qquad\text{provided that }P(B)>0
$$

**Read aloud:** “The probability of A given B is the probability of A and B jointly, divided by the probability of B.” Here the vertical bar means “given”, not division. In the die example, $P(A\mid B)=\frac{\frac13}{\frac12}=\frac23$. If $P(B)=0$, this quotient is **not** defined; elementary conditional probability must not be calculated in this way. More general conditional distributions are an advanced concept.

**Independence** is stronger than merely having a small correlation: two events are independent if $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\cdot P(B)$. If B has positive probability, it follows that $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. This is not true of the events in the die example, because $\frac13$ is not equal to $\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14$.

**Bayes’ formula as a change of perspective:**

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\cdot P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

**Read aloud:** “A given B equals B given A times A, divided by B.” The formula rearranges the same joint probability. In AI classification it is helpful when the probability of an alarm **given a fault** is known, but we want to know how likely a **fault is given an alarm**. The base rate of faults is also required.

**Practical example:** Only 1% of all files contain an error. A checker detects 90% of erroneous files and falsely raises an alarm for 5% of intact files. Among 10,000 files, we expect about 90 true alarms (out of 100 erroneous files) and 495 false alarms (out of 9,900 intact files). Thus, of 585 alarms, only 90 correspond to actual errors: $\frac{90}{585}\approx15{,}4\,\%$ (the exact value is $\frac{90}{585}=\frac{2}{13}$). These are **modelled expected frequencies**; the observed counts for 10,000 actual random files need not be exactly the same. A high detection rate for errors does not automatically mean high reliability *of a positive alarm*.

## Random variable, distribution and expected value {#S6}

A random variable $X$ assigns a numerical value to each elementary outcome. In a **general probability space**, it must also be *measurable*: for every real threshold, the event “$X$ is at or below this threshold” belongs to the admissible sigma-algebra. Otherwise its probability would not necessarily be defined. In our finite examples with the power set as the system of events, this condition is automatically satisfied. The **distribution** specifies the probabilities with which the values occur. For discrete variables, the probabilities of individual values can be listed.

For discrete values $x_i$ with probabilities $p_i$, we have:

$$
E[X]=\sum_i x_i\cdot p_i
$$

**Read aloud:** “The expected value of X is the sum over all values i of x sub i times p sub i.” The subscript $i$ is a running index; $p_i$ stands for $P(X=x_i)$. The weights $p_i$ are non-negative and sum to one. With infinitely many possible values, the expected value is guaranteed to be defined as a finite number only under suitable summability conditions; for example, it suffices that the sum of the absolute values $\lvert x_i\rvert\cdot p_i$ is finite.

**Worked example:** A workload takes the values 0, 1 or 2 with probabilities 0.2, 0.5 and 0.3. Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=0\cdot0{,}2+1\cdot0{,}5+2\cdot0{,}3=1{,}1$. This does not mean that 1.1 tasks can occur in one trial, but describes the long-term mean under the model.

The **variance** measures the mean squared deviation from the expected value:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[{(X-E[X])}^2]
$$

**Read aloud:** “The variance of X is the expected value of the square of the deviation of X from its own expected value.” In the example, $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0^2\cdot0{,}2+1^2\cdot0{,}5+2^2\cdot0{,}3=1{,}7$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=1{,}7-{1{,}1}^2=0{,}49$. The standard deviation is its square root: $\sigma=0{,}7$ tasks. It has the same unit as the original random variable.

## Continuous distributions: density is not probability {#S7}

For a random variable **that can be described by a density**, we consider a **density** $f_X$ instead of individual point probabilities. Not every distribution is either purely discrete or has a density: mixtures and singular distributions are also possible. The probability between two bounds is obtained by **integrating** the density; the distribution function $F_X(t)$ gives the probability of obtaining at most the value $t$.

$$
P(a\leq X\leq b)=\int_a^b f_X(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
$$

**Read aloud:** “The probability that X lies between a and b is the integral of the density f sub X from a to b with respect to t.” The variable $t$ is merely the variable of integration here. For the formula to hold, $f_X$ must be non-negative and integrate to one over the entire real line; for $a\le b$ the endpoints then make no difference. For a distribution described by an ordinary density, $P(X=a)=0$, even if the density is high at that point. The normal distribution is a common example of a continuous distribution, but by no means every data phenomenon is normally distributed.

## Statistics: drawing conclusions about unknown properties from data {#S8}

The **sample mean** $\bar x$ is the sum of the observed values divided by their number $n$. It is an estimate of the unknown theoretical expected value $\mu$, provided the data collection process is suitable. For independent and identically distributed observations with a finite expected value, the law of large numbers describes the long-term convergence of the sample mean towards $\mu$. This does **not** mean that every finite sample must be close to the true mean. With finite variance $\sigma^2$, the sample mean has variance $\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$ and standard error $\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$; in the presence of dependencies this formula is generally not correct.

A **confidence interval** is calculated from random data according to a specified rule. Under the model assumptions of a 95% method, about 95% of these intervals should cover the fixed unknown parameter over very many repetitions. This is **not** a statement that, after observing a particular frequentist interval, the already fixed parameter lies within it with probability 95%. Unreliable or biased samples do not automatically become correct just because the interval is narrow.

**Worked confidence interval:** Suppose we have 100 independent normally distributed measurements, the population standard deviation is **known** to be $\sigma=2$, and the observed mean is $\bar x=10$. Then the standard error is $\frac{2}{\sqrt{100}}=0{,}2$. Under the normal model the two-sided 95% interval is $10\pm1{,}96\cdot0{,}2$, or **$[9{,}608;\,10{,}392]$**. If the standard deviation is unknown or other assumptions apply, this simple normal formula cannot be used without further justification.

In a **hypothesis test**, $H_0$ denotes a null hypothesis. Under that null model, a p-value is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the observed one according to the chosen test statistic criterion. It is **not** the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor a measure of the practical magnitude of an effect. Study design, multiple testing, assumptions and the selection of measurements are crucial. A level $\alpha=0{,}05$ is often specified before the test. The rule $p\le\alpha$ controls the type I error **under the valid null hypothesis and test assumptions**, not the probability of error *for this particular significant result*. When several unplanned tests are performed, the risk of at least one false alarm increases without correction.

## What is it useful for? {#S9}

- **AI and automatic classification:** Prior probabilities, sensitivity and false alarm rates determine how informative positive results are.
- **Software benchmarks:** Expected value and dispersion distinguish typical running times from unstable outliers; appropriate sampling design prevents false certainty.
- **Robotics and sensor fusion:** Measurement noise can be modelled as a random variable; its distribution determines how uncertain a position estimate is.
- **Simulation:** Artificial data are generated from theoretical distributions to estimate possible system states and risks.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S10}

- A distribution is a **model assumption**, not an automatic finding. Real measurements may be dependent, skewed or unrepresentative.
- Conditional probabilities must not be interchanged: $P(A\mid B)$ and $P(B\mid A)$ generally have entirely different values.
- A mean does not describe a guaranteed individual output; a small sample can fluctuate considerably.
- Correlation does not establish causation. In particular, third variables must be considered in observational data.
- In continuous models, **density values** must be distinguished from **probabilities over intervals**.
