# M2-L13 – Logic, computability and theoretical computer science {#L13}

**Course area:** M2 – Advanced Bachelor’s studies / transition to Master’s studies

**Level:** Bachelor’s / bridge to Master’s studies

**Prior knowledge:** No fixed prerequisites specified.

**Context:** University – advanced theoretical studies

## What is this about? {#S1}

Computers process symbols according to rules. But when can we prove that a procedure does the right thing? Which problems can in principle be solved by an algorithm, and for which problems can there be no general decision algorithm? Mathematical logic describes propositions, inferences and proofs; theoretical computer science investigates formal languages, computational models, running time and computability. The surprising limitation is that not every precisely formulated problem is decidable by a general program. This does not, however, mean that computers have only limited practical capabilities.

Connections: M1-L1 (logic, quantifiers and proofs), M2-L1 (graphs and algorithms), M2-L3 (algebraic structures), M2-L10 (coding), M2-L11 (AI) and M2-L12 (robot control as an algorithm).

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

In everyday language, we call many things ‘computable’ if software sometimes finds an answer. Mathematics makes finer distinctions:

- **Syntax:** Which strings count as well-formed formulae or programs in the first place?
- **Semantics:** What do such formulae mean, and when are they true?
- **Provability:** Which propositions follow from specified assumptions by admissible rules of inference?
- **Decidability:** Is there an algorithm that returns the correct yes/no answer after finitely many steps for **every** permitted input?
- **Complexity:** How rapidly do time or memory requirements increase as inputs become larger?

A mathematical proposition may be true even though a particular proof system cannot prove it. A problem may be decidable even though we know no practically efficient algorithm for it. And a program may seem correct on a great many examples without having a general proof of correctness.

## Propositional logic: reasoning from truth values {#S4}

In classical logic, a **proposition** is a linguistic or formal sentence with a definite truth value: true or false. $P$ and $Q$ might, for example, mean ‘The sensor reports an obstacle’ and ‘The robot stops’. From these we form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\land Q&\quad\text{both propositions are true}\\
P\lor Q&\quad\text{at least one of the two propositions is true}\\
\neg P&\quad\text{P is not true}\\
P\implies Q&\quad\text{if P is true, Q must also be true}
\end{aligned}
$$

**First formula read in full:** ‘P and Q’ is true if and only if both propositions are true. The second reads ‘P or Q’ and uses **inclusive** or: the combination is true even when both propositions are true. The third reads ‘not P’. The fourth reads ‘P implies Q’; as a material implication it is false only when $P$ is true but $Q$ is false.

Why this last truth table? An implication excludes the case ‘premise true, asserted consequence false’. When the premise is false, this counterexample does not arise. It does **not** follow that the implication describes an actual causal mechanism.

A valid inference pattern is called **modus ponens**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\begin{gathered}P\\P\implies Q\end{gathered}}{Q}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘P holds. P implies Q. Therefore Q holds.’ If the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false in classical logic. But from $P \Rightarrow  Q$ and $Q$ alone, we must **not** conclude $P$. For example: if it rains, the road gets wet; but a road may also become wet as a result of cleaning.

### Truth table for implication: four cases {#abschnitt-5}

In classical logic, the proposition $P \Rightarrow  Q$ is equivalent to $\neg P \lor  Q$. It says nothing about a temporal or causal relationship. The complete check is:

| P | Q | $P \Rightarrow  Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| true | true | true |
| true | false | **false** |
| false | true | true |
| false | false | true |

**Interpretation:** There is exactly one counterexample: the premise holds, but the consequence does not. By contrast, the converse $Q \Rightarrow  P$ is a **different** proposition. The truth of an implication does not automatically make its converse true.

## Predicate logic and the order of quantifiers {#S5}

Propositional logic deals with complete propositions. **Predicate logic** can speak about objects and their properties. Consider a proposition about natural numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\forall n\in\mathbb N:\ \exists m\in\mathbb N:\ m>n
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud in full:** ‘For every natural number n, there exists at least one natural number m that is greater than n.’ A simple argument takes a given natural number $n$ and chooses the number $m=n+1$. This proves the proposition regardless of whether zero is included among the natural numbers.

The order of the quantifiers is essential. Reversing them gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\exists m\in\mathbb N:\ \forall n\in\mathbb N:\ m>n
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘There exists a single natural number m that is greater than every natural number n.’ This is false. For every fixed number $m$, $m+1$ is still greater. In the first sentence, the choice of $m$ may depend on $n$; in the second, **one and the same** $m$ must suffice for all numbers.

This makes a considerable difference in software requirements: ‘For every request there exists a suitable response’ does not mean ‘There is a single response for all requests’.

## Formal proofs: separating syntax and meaning {#S6}

A **formal system** specifies which strings are formulae, which axioms are admissible and which rules of inference generate new formulae. The notation $A \vdash  B$ means ‘B is formally derivable from the assumptions A’. The notation $A \models  B$ means ‘B is true in every model under consideration in which A is true’. A good calculus connects the two through properties such as **soundness** (provability implies semantic validity) and, where applicable, **completeness** (semantic validity implies provability). Whether completeness holds depends on the **particular calculus and its domain**.

An example of a mathematical proof is induction. Consider the sum of the first $n$ positive odd numbers:

$$
1+3+5+\cdots+(2n-1)=n^2
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘The sum of the first n positive odd numbers is n squared.’ This is correct for $n=1$: the left-hand side is `1`, and the right-hand side is $1^{2}=1$.

Assume the proposition is true for a fixed positive natural number $n$. Adding the next odd number $2n+1$ gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[1+3+\ldots+(2n-1)]+(2n+1)&=n^2+2n+1\\
&={(n+1)}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud:** ‘Adding the next odd number to the already assumed sum n squared gives exactly the square of n plus one.’ This proves the transition from $n$ to $n+1$. Together with the base case, the proposition is proved for all positive natural numbers. The role of the assumption is important: in the **induction step**, it applies to an arbitrary fixed $n$, not as an unjustified assumption of the entire result.

A proof is more than checking a few examples. Ideally, it covers **all** admissible inputs of a mathematically specified problem.

## Formal languages and finite automata {#S7}

An **alphabet** $\Sigma$ is a finite set of symbols. In the following example, we choose $\Sigma =\{0,1\}$. $\Sigma \ast$ is the set of **all finite strings** over these two symbols, including the empty word $\varepsilon$ of length zero. A **formal language** is a selected subset of these words. For example, $L$ could consist of all bit strings containing an even number of ones.

A **deterministic finite automaton** has finitely many states and a transition rule $\delta$. It reads the word from left to right, one symbol at a time, and changes state according to that rule. We use two states: $s_{0}$ means ‘an even number of ones so far’, and $s_{1}$ means ‘an odd number of ones so far’. We start in $s_{0}$. A zero does not change the parity; a one switches it.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta(s_0,0)&=s_0,\quad\delta(s_0,1)=s_1\\
\delta(s_1,0)&=s_1,\quad\delta(s_1,1)=s_0
\end{aligned}
$$

**Read aloud in full:** ‘If we read a zero in state s zero, we stay in s zero. If we read a one there, we switch to s one. In state s one, a zero has no effect on parity; a one takes us back to s zero.’ We accept the word if and only if the final state is $s_{0}$.

**Fully worked example:** For the word `1010`, we start in $s_{0}$. The first one takes us to $s_{1}$. The following zero leaves us in $s_{1}$. The next one takes us back to $s_{0}$; the final zero leaves us there. Thus `1010` contains two ones and is accepted. By contrast, `1011` contains three ones and ends in $s_{1}$; it is not accepted.

The pattern can be decided for **every finite bit string** without storing the entire text: only the state recording parity is needed. This simplicity makes finite automata useful for protocols, text patterns and many control states.

### Why two automaton states are really necessary {#abschnitt-9}

The automaton for an even number of ones accepts the **empty word** $\varepsilon$, since it contains zero ones and zero is even. It also accepts `00` and `11`, but not `1` or `101`. The states $s_{0}$ and $s_{1}$ cannot be merged without losing information: without reading another symbol, the word that is empty so far must be accepted, but the word `1` must be rejected. A single state of a deterministic finite automaton could not distinguish these two cases. Thus two states are **minimal** for this example.

## Turing machines: a more general computational model {#S8}

A finite automaton has only finitely many states and no arbitrarily large working memory. A **Turing machine** is an abstract computational model with finite control and a potentially unbounded tape on which symbols can be read, written and accessed step by step. Any individual computation uses only finitely many tape cells actually reached if it halts after finitely many steps.

The model is not a blueprint for a particular modern CPU. It describes which computational steps can be executed by **finite, mechanically specified rules**. Other known formalisms, such as lambda calculus and recursive functions, have the same expressive power with respect to computable functions; their mathematical equivalence can be proved.

The **Church–Turing thesis** goes a step further: it identifies the informal notion of an ‘effectively computable procedure’ with the functions formally computable in such a model. Because an ‘effective procedure’ is initially an **informal** concept, this is not a theorem that could be proved within a single formalism. This must be distinguished from the equivalence of precisely defined models.

## Decidable, recognisable and undecidable {#S9}

A **decision problem** asks for a yes or no answer for an input. It is **decidable** if an algorithm returns the correct answer after finitely many steps for every admissible input. A problem is **semidecidable** or **recognisable** if a procedure eventually halts and reports ‘yes’ on every yes input; on a no input, it may continue indefinitely.

An example of a recognisable problem is ‘Will a given program eventually halt on this particular input?’ We can run the program. If it halts, we have found the yes answer. If it has not halted so far, however, this does **not** tell us whether it will halt later.

The generalised **halting problem** demands more: a universal algorithm that correctly returns yes or no after finitely many steps for **every** program and every input. No such algorithm exists.

## Why there is no general halting decider {#S10}

The basic idea of the impossibility proof is a **contradiction through self-application**. Suppose hypothetically that there were a perfect program $H(M,w)$. For every program text $M$ and every input $w$, it would say in finite time whether $M$ halts on $w$. Here, $H$ would stand for ‘halting decider’, **not** Shannon entropy from M2-L10.

From this supposedly perfect decider, one could construct a new program $D$:

Formula, diagram or calculation scheme · original notation

    Program D(P):
        if H(P,P) reports ‘halts’:
            run forever
        else:
            halt immediately

**Read aloud:** ‘Program D receives a program text P. It asks the assumed halting decider whether P halts when applied to itself. If the decider reports yes, D deliberately runs forever. If the decider reports no, D halts immediately.’ Under the assumption of an always-halting $H$, this instruction is a precisely executable computational procedure.

Now we give $D$ **its own program text** as input: $D(D)$. There are only two cases:

1.  $H(D,D)$ claims that $D(D)$ halts. By the construction of $D$, it then runs **forever** instead. The claim of $H$ would be false.
2.  $H(D,D)$ claims that $D(D)$ does not halt. By construction, $D$ then halts **immediately** instead. This claim would also be false.

Both cases contradict the assumed correctness of $H$. **Conclusion:** A general halting decider that always halts and is always correct for arbitrary programs and inputs cannot exist.

The proof does **not** mean that we can never determine whether a particular program halts. For a great many restricted programs and languages, such statements are decidable or provable. The theorem concerns the unrestricted universal problem.

## Decidability and computational cost are different questions {#S11}

A second limitation arises even for decidable problems: the **cost** may grow rapidly with the input size. The Landau notation $O(n)$ means roughly that, for sufficiently large inputs, the cost is bounded above by a constant times $n$. For example, $O(n^{2})$ means that growth is bounded above by a multiple of the square of the input size. This notation makes **no** claim about a specific number of seconds on a real computer.

For a graph with $V$ vertices and $E$ edges, an appropriate breadth-first search in a standard adjacency-list representation can take $O(V+E)$ time. **Read aloud:** ‘The time cost is at most on the order of the number of vertices plus the number of edges.’ These quantities are not simply the number of all possible paths. M2-L1 explains why the state space can nevertheless become very large.

In simplified terms, the complexity class **P** contains problems decidable by deterministic algorithms in polynomial time. **NP** contains decision problems whose yes certificates can be verified by a deterministic algorithm in polynomial time; an equivalent definition uses nondeterministic computation. Thus $\mathrm{NP}$ does **not** mean ‘not polynomial’. $P$ is a subclass of $\mathrm{NP}$; whether the classes are actually different is a fundamental open question. The classical satisfiability problem for propositional formulae (SAT) is **NP-complete**, but *decidable*. The undecidability of the halting problem and the high cost of SAT are therefore **fundamentally different statements**.

## Gödel’s incompleteness: a different limitation {#S12}

A **formal proof system** can be consistent and have algorithmically enumerable axioms, yet be unable to prove all mathematical truths within its domain. In simplified terms, Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem says that in a **sufficiently strong**, consistent and effectively axiomatisable formal system capable of expressing basic arithmetic, certain propositions about natural numbers remain undecidable **within that system**. The precise technical formulation requires careful conditions, including the strength of the system and the notions of truth and proof being used.

Three things must be distinguished here:

- **A particular system cannot prove a theorem.** This is a statement about its formal proof capabilities.
- **An algorithm cannot decide a general problem.** This concerns an entire problem and every admissible input.
- **A practical program takes too much computation time.** This is a question of available resources, not necessarily of impossibility in principle.

These limitations are related but not identical. Nor does Gödel’s result mean that ordinary school mathematics is unreliable or that every proposition can be turned into an undecidable contradiction.

## Consequences for software, AI and robotics {#S13}

In software, program properties can be checked automatically for **suitable, restricted models**. Finite automata and finite-state models, for example, are suitable for model checking. For more complex systems, a proof assistant helps mechanically verify a formal proof developed with human involvement. Such a proof is always relative to the stated axioms, definitions, modelling assumptions and the correctness of the tools used.

A language model such as that in M2-L11 can propose plausible proof steps or programs. Plausibility, however, is not formal validity. For safety-critical control and robotics (M2-L12), verifiable specifications, tests, simulations, limit monitoring and, where appropriate, formal proofs are useful. The halting problem does not prohibit extensive analysis of individual real programs; it shows why a perfect automated procedure for **all** arbitrary programs cannot be expected.

## What is this useful for? {#S14}

Logic and computability help in the design of programming languages, static analyses, formal specifications, verification tools and AI systems. Finite automata model protocols and process states; propositional logic and SAT tools support planning and consistency checks. Complexity theory distinguishes ‘theoretically possible’ from ‘practical within a reasonable time’. Undecidability theory prevents false expectations of universal software verification tools.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S15}

We predominantly use **classical two-valued logic**. Other logics have somewhat different rules of inference. The halting proof assumes sufficiently general program encoding and self-application. Complexity classes refer to precisely defined decision problems and computational models; time estimates abstract away from specific hardware. Gödel’s results require a strong formal system and must not be generalised to arbitrary collections of axioms. A universal statement about algorithms is not a statement about every individual concrete machine.
