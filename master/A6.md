# Study levels – from school through Bachelor’s to Master’s

## Purpose and limits of these statements {#S1}

**Bachelor’s** and **Master’s** refer to qualification levels of degree programmes, not to properties of individual mathematical concepts. The same theory may be introduced in a Bachelor's elective module and explored at research level in a Master's seminar. The classification below is therefore an **editorially designed learning path**, not a certificate, an ECTS equivalent or a set of study or examination regulations. In particular, the availability of a teaching text does not imply completion of a degree programme in the subject or the award of an academic degree.

## Overview of the five stages {#S2}

| Stage | Educational level | Status | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| School M1-L1–M1-L23 | Lower secondary education | 23/23 | Foundations and mathematical language |
| School M2-L1–M2-L17 | Upper secondary education (Gymnasium) | 17/17 | Calculus, geometry, probability and statistics |
| M1-L1–M1-L9 | **Bachelor’s – core studies** | 9/9 | Proofs, analysis, linear algebra, probability and statistics, numerical mathematics |
| M2-L1–M2-L13 | **Advanced Bachelor’s ↔ bridge to Master’s** | 13/13 | Broad range of advanced and applied topics at varying levels of depth |
| M3-L1–M3-L12 | **Master’s – advanced theory and methods** | 12/12 | Generalisation, conditions, proof ideas, more demanding models |

The lessons in module M3 require appropriate prior knowledge in each case, not necessarily a complete sequential reading of school mathematics and university modules M1–M2. They are designed as **readable, systematic introductions** and cannot replace a regular Master's-level course with specialist literature, seminars, independent proof work and examinations.

## What distinguishes the transition to Master’s level in this course? {#S3}

- **Bachelor’s-oriented:** Build up fundamental concepts, explain standard results, become familiar with proof ideas and work through complete computational examples.
- **Master’s-oriented:** Explicitly check assumptions, understand counterexamples, connect several theories and explain the limits of algorithms or solution methods.
- **Scientific practice:** Distinguish sources, model assumptions, open questions and validity; a formal Master's degree requires substantially more than reading this archive.

**Example:** M1-L3 asks how the integral is calculated; M2-L7 explains the Lebesgue integral and $L^2$; M3-L1 examines when the order of a limit and an integral may be interchanged and demonstrates a case where this does not work. M1-L6 covers matrix eigenvalues; M3-L2 distinguishes eigenvalues from the spectrum of an operator on an infinite-dimensional space. M2-L8 introduces heat equations; M3-L3 formulates them in weak form and shows an initial finite element approximation.

## Orientation based on example degree programmes {#S6}

- **Freie Universität Berlin** describes its Master's programme in mathematics as research-oriented and lists fields of study including (applied) analysis, numerical mathematics, geometry, probability and statistics, topology and number theory.
- **Technische Universität Berlin** describes elective options and areas of specialisation in numerical mathematics, differential equations/functional analysis, probability and statistics, geometry, and algebra/discrete mathematics. Advanced mathematical modules and seminars are part of the programme structure.
- The twelve lessons M3-L1–M3-L12 are **not a copy of a module handbook**, but an independently compiled advanced learning path with a strong focus on technology, AI and robotics.

## Quality and limitations {#S7}

The lessons provide an introduction. Supplementary lectures, exercises and specialist literature are useful for reaching the depth of a university module. Errors in subject matter are possible. Individual topics can be looked up in the formula register and the semantic formula catalogue.

## Overview of advanced Master’s-level and compulsory elective topics {#S8}

The advanced lessons in module M3 cover subjects including measure theory and functional analysis, stochastic processes, optimisation, numerical mathematics, geometry and dynamics. The lessons in module M4 offer **optional perspectives on specialist topics**, for example AI, cryptography, privacy protection, robotics and distributed systems. They are not official university module requirements.

A sensible starting path leads from school mathematics via M1 to relevant topics in M2 and M3, and only then to a chosen specialisation in M4. The sequence and prior knowledge required depend on the respective topic.
