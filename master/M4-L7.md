# M4-L7 – Compulsory elective: Differential privacy and federated learning {#L7}

**Course area:** M4 – Choice of study: AI, computer science and specialised fields

**Level:** University studies (elective / specialisation)

**Prior knowledge:** [M1-L7](M1-L7.md) (probability), [M3-L5](M3-L5.md) (statistics), [M3-L6](M3-L6.md) (optimisation), [M2-L11](M2-L11.md) (AI mathematics), [M4-L5](M4-L5.md) (security objectives)

**Context:** Master's-oriented specialisation in computer science and AI; also accessible to advanced Bachelor's students with a background in statistics.

## What is this about? {#S1}

Even when a dataset is published only in the form of an average, a frequency or a trained AI model, information about individuals may be revealed. **Differential privacy (DP)** therefore asks more than whether names have been removed. Instead, it formulates a **mathematical guarantee about the random behaviour of an output procedure**: the statistical change in an outcome should be limited in a controlled way when one person's data is absent or replaced. **Federated learning** takes a different approach: training data remains on devices or within institutions, while selected model updates are combined. The two approaches can be combined, but they are not the same.

## Basic idea and intuition {#S2}

A research team wants to count how many users have activated a feature. Publishing an exact number could reveal one individual's participation by comparing two datasets. The difference between the two datasets here is just one vote. If the team returns a **randomly perturbed number**, the two possible underlying situations become harder to distinguish. However, the random perturbation must be calibrated to the maximum sensitivity of the query, and each additional query may reveal further information.

A second team wants to improve a predictive model on many phones. Devices can calculate gradients locally instead of sending raw data to a central computer. However, **gradients can also contain private information**. Federated learning alone therefore provides no differential privacy guarantee. This requires a specifically justified and correctly implemented DP mechanism.

## Defining privacy mathematically {#S4}

For two **neighbouring** datasets $D$ and $D'$ and every set of possible outputs $S$, a random mechanism $M$ is called **$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differentially private** if:

$$
\Pr[M(D)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr[M(D\prime)\in S]+\delta
$$

**Read in full:** The probability that the mechanism produces an output from set S when using dataset D is at most e to the power of epsilon times the corresponding probability for the neighbouring dataset D prime, plus delta. The inequality must hold for **every** permitted neighbouring pair and **every** output set. Since it also applies to the pair in reverse order, the comparison is controlled in both directions.

When $\delta  = 0$, we speak of *pure epsilon-DP*. A smaller $\varepsilon$ limits distinguishability more strongly; however, the numerical values can only be interpreted in conjunction with the adjacency model, data flow, mechanism, publication frequency and adversary model. $\delta$ is **not** simply the probability that “all data is disclosed”. That would be an incorrect simplification of the definition.

There are two important adjacency models: **adding/removing one person** (*add/remove*) and **replacing one contribution** (*replace-one*). They can lead to different sensitivities. Before performing a calculation, we must establish which model applies.

## Sensitivity and the Laplace mechanism {#S5}

A numerical function $f(D)$ is to be published. Its **global absolute sensitivity** is:

$$
\Delta_1f=\sup_{D,D\prime\;\mathrm{neighbouring}}\lvert f(D)-f(D\prime)\rvert
$$

This means: how much can the number change in the worst permitted neighbouring case? **$\sup$** is read as “supremum”: the least upper bound of all possible changes. In finite examples, it is often actually a maximum; in more general ranges, this maximum need not be attained. When counting individual contributions, each equal to either zero or one, the sensitivity for the replacement case used here is $\Delta _{1} f = 1$.

A classical mechanism adds Laplace noise:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M(D)&=f(D)+Z\\
Z&\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,b),\quad\text{with }b=\frac{\Delta_1 f}{\varepsilon}
\quad\text{for }\varepsilon>0
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: the output consists of the exact count plus a randomly sampled Laplace value. The scale b is the maximum change divided by the privacy parameter epsilon. The Laplace density is symmetric about zero; large deviations become exponentially less frequent as their magnitude increases. Under the stated conditions, this mechanism satisfies pure $\varepsilon$-DP.

**Complete numerical example:** Twelve people have activated a feature. The neighbouring dataset contains eleven such activations. The published count has a sensitivity of one. For demonstration, choose $\varepsilon  = \ln (2) \approx  0{,}693$, giving $b=\frac1{\ln(2)}\approx1{,}443$. The maximum factor in the DP inequality is now $e^\varepsilon=2$. This does **not** mean that each individual published count can deviate by at most two; Laplace noise is unbounded in both directions. It means that there is a precise bound on **probability ratios for output sets**. If the sampled noise value were $Z = -1{,}2$, the published number would be $12 - 1{,}2 = 10{,}8$. Publishing a value rounded to a whole number would constitute post-processing; it does not weaken the DP guarantee for the same publication.

**Assumption:** The count is the only protected release; other releases, participant selection and internal access must be considered separately. Revealing the exact number of participants may also violate the privacy assumptions if that number is itself private.

**Exact ratio cross-check:** For two neighbouring counts $f(D)=12$ and $f(D')=11$, the Laplace mechanism with $b=\frac1{\ln(2)}$ has the following two densities at a possible real-valued output $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{12}(x)&=\frac1{2b}\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{|x-12|}{b}\right)\\
p_{11}(x)&=\frac1{2b}\cdot\exp\!\left(-\frac{|x-11|}{b}\right)\\
\frac{p_{12}(x)}{p_{11}(x)}&=\exp\!\left(\frac{|x-11|-|x-12|}{b}\right)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

For $x=12$, the ratio is exactly $\exp\left(\frac1b\right)=2$; for $x=11$, however, it is $\exp\left(-\frac1b\right)=\frac12$. For **every** real $x$, the triangle inequality $||x-11|-|x-12|| \le  1$ shows that the ratio lies between $\frac12$ and $2$. Integrating over arbitrary output sets establishes the two-sided pure $\ln (2)$-DP bound for this neighbouring pair. **Important:** A density value is not the probability of exactly one real-valued output point. The privacy definition concerns **sets** of outputs, not isolated density values.

## Means, norm bounds and post-processing {#S6}

The sensitivity of a mean can only be bounded straightforwardly if the range of values and the adjacency rule are established. Example: there are **exactly ten fixed** contributions, and each value is guaranteed to lie between zero and one. When one contribution is **replaced**, the mean changes by at most $\frac1{10}=0{,}1$. If the number of participants is secret or variable, however, this small formula is **not** sufficient without further specifications.

One fundamental principle is known as *post-processing*: if a DP output alone is processed further, without accessing the secret original data again, this subsequent processing does not weaken the mathematically guaranteed DP bound. A chart derived from noisy numbers is protected in this sense; renewed access to precise raw data is not automatically protected.

## Privacy budget and repeated queries {#S7}

Multiple outputs may complement one another. Under simple **sequential composition** of two DP mechanisms on the same protected data, we obtain the following general upper bound:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\varepsilon_1,\delta_1)\text{ combined with }(\varepsilon_2,\delta_2)\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2,\delta_1+\delta_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: the two epsilon bounds are added, as are the two delta bounds. This is a conservative bound that also applies when later queries are selected adaptively in an appropriate manner. For $\varepsilon _{1} = 0{,}4$ and $\varepsilon _{2} = 0{,}3$, the composed epsilon value is at most $0{,}7$; if both mechanisms satisfy pure DP, delta remains zero. More advanced methods can provide better accounting, **but not every additional query is free**. In operation, budget accounting, the mechanism version and the specification of a protected unit are required.

## Federated learning as a different building block {#S8}

In federated learning, several devices have local datasets. Each trains a model to some extent and sends **model updates** for aggregation. A simple weighted average is:

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{n_1\cdot\theta_1+n_2\cdot\theta_2}{n_1+n_2}
$$

Read aloud: the new shared parameter vector is the sum of the two local parameter vectors, each weighted by the number of local examples, divided by the total number of examples. This is a simplified *federated averaging* principle; real systems synchronise multiple local training steps and repeated rounds.

**Worked example:** Device A has $n_{1} = 20$ examples and reports the vector $\theta_1=(1;2)$. Device B has $n_{2} = 30$ examples and reports $\theta_2=(3;0)$. Component by component, we obtain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{\mathrm{new}}&=\frac{20\cdot(1;2)+30\cdot(3;0)}{50}\\
&=\frac{(20;40)+(90;0)}{50}\\
&=\frac{(110;40)}{50}\\
&=(2{,}2;0{,}8)
\end{aligned}
$$

The shared parameter vector is therefore $(2{,}2;0{,}8)$. The calculation proves **no privacy guarantee**. The parameters could reveal information about individual training examples.

## Clipping, secure aggregation and DP training {#S9}

Before applying a DP mechanism, update norms are often bounded so that individual participants have a controllable influence:

$$
g_{\mathrm{clipped}}=g\cdot\min\left(1,\frac C{\lVert g\rVert_2}\right)
$$

The formula means: if the vector is shorter than the bound C, it remains unchanged. Otherwise, it is shortened to length C. Example: $g=(3;4)$ has a norm of five. For $C = 2$, we obtain $g_{\mathrm{clipped}}=(3;4)\cdot\left(\frac25\right)=(1{,}2;1{,}6)$, whose norm is exactly two. Clipping alone is **not yet a DP mechanism**: it lacks the correctly calibrated random perturbation of the published aggregation.

**Secure aggregation**, in turn, aims to prevent the server from seeing individual local updates in plaintext; it uses cryptography and addresses a different risk from differential privacy. *Client-level DP* protects the participation of an entire device or person; *example-level DP* instead protects individual training examples. This choice changes sensitivity, sampling, budget accounting and the guarantee that must be justified.

**Caution:** A formula for Laplace noise applied to a simple count cannot be transferred unchanged to a complex, multi-stage training process. In DP-SGD, per-example gradients are typically clipped, aggregated and perturbed with appropriately calibrated noise, while a privacy accountant tracks the multiple training steps. A fully correct guarantee and implementation require additional assumptions.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

- **AI training with sensitive data:** quantify privacy bounds for participation or individual training examples instead of merely claiming that data has been “anonymised”.
- **Smartphones and distributed devices:** train local models without storing all raw data centrally; combine with DP and secure aggregation when needed.
- **Official statistics:** publish noisy frequencies and means, provided that adjacency, value ranges and the overall budget are correctly specified.
- **Security analysis:** examine whether private information also leaks through logs, metadata, variable participant numbers or faulty implementations.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

1.  **DP is a statement about a specified random mechanism**, not about the ethical quality or general legal compliance of the data processing. Consent, data minimisation and secure storage remain relevant even with DP.
2.  Epsilon values are hardly comparable without the adjacency unit, delta, sampling, composition and mechanism. Small epsilon values improve the defined bound but may reduce the usefulness of the data.
3.  **Federated learning is no substitute for DP.** Updates, timing, participant lists and metadata can reveal information; secure aggregation covers other risks.
4.  Clipping may introduce bias into data and models. With unbounded data, a formally claimed sensitivity may be incorrect.
5.  Implementation errors, unprotected intermediate results and subsequent releases can render a theoretically sound calculation ineffective in practice.
