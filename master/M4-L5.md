# M4-L5 – Compulsory elective: Modern cryptography and post-quantum methods {#L5}

**Course area:** M4 – Choice of study: AI, computer science and specialised fields

**Level:** University studies (elective / specialisation)

**Prior knowledge:** [M2-L3](M2-L3.md) (groups, fields, modular arithmetic), [M2-L13](M2-L13.md) (algorithms and computability), [M1-L7](M1-L7.md) (probability), [M2-L10](M2-L10.md) (bits, information and coding)

**Context:** Computer science / mathematics specialisation, from advanced Bachelor’s to Master’s level.

## What is this about? {#S1}

Cryptography protects messages, keys and identities against certain types of attack. Security does not arise from keeping a calculation secret, but from well-understood mathematical properties, carefully designed protocols and suitable secrets. Classical asymmetric methods often rely on assumptions about the difficulty of factorisation or discrete logarithms. Large fault-tolerant quantum computers could threaten some of these foundations. **Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)** develops algorithms intended to remain secure even against known quantum attacks – without claiming that every conceivable future attack is ruled out.

## Basic idea: three different security objectives {#S2}

- **Confidentiality:** Unauthorised parties should not be able to read a message.
- **Integrity:** Tampering should be detected.
- **Authenticity:** The receiving party should be able to verify the claimed communication partner or signer.

A **digital signature** does not encrypt a message. A **key encapsulation mechanism (KEM)** does not simply transmit a message of arbitrary length; it enables the joint derivation of a secret key, which can then be used, for example, for authenticated symmetric encryption. These functions must be combined correctly within the overall system.

## Symmetric encryption: a transparent bit-level example {#S4}

In **symmetric encryption**, the sender and receiver share a secret key. XOR illustrates the idea using a single bit pattern and a key of the same length:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Message }m&=1010\\
\text{Key }k&=0110\\
\text{Ciphertext }c&=1100\quad\text{(bitwise XOR)}\\
\text{Decryption: }\quad 1100\oplus0110&=1010\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “XOR gives one precisely when the two input bits differ. Applying the same key again recovers the original message.” A **one-time pad** would require a key that is as long as the message, truly random, secret and used **exactly once**. Under these conditions, information-theoretic secrecy is achieved. Reusing keys destroys this guarantee.

Practical systems do not use hand-generated XOR patterns, but well-tested methods for **authenticated encryption**, such as AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305, together with correct key and nonce management. A nonce is a value selected in a controlled manner for each encryption context; improper reuse can severely undermine security. One should not invent one's own “lightweight” cryptography for production data.

## Asymmetric mathematics: RSA solely as an illustrative calculation {#S5}

A **public** key pair consists of a part that may be published and a secret part. In the classical school-level RSA example, we choose **tiny, deliberately insecure** prime numbers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p&=5,\quad q=11\text{.}\\
n&=p\cdot q=55\text{.}\\
\varphi(n)&=(p-1)(q-1)=4\cdot10=40\text{.}\\
e&=3,\quad\text{since }\gcd(3,40)=1\text{.}\\
d&=27,\quad\text{since }3\cdot27=81\equiv1\pmod{40}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “The product of five and eleven is fifty-five. The totient function gives forty. Three and forty are coprime. The private exponent twenty-seven is chosen so that three times twenty-seven leaves remainder one when divided by forty.”

We encode an admissible small message as $m=7$ and use the bare mathematical RSA exponentiation for illustration:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Encryption: }\quad c&=m^e\bmod n=7^3\bmod55\\
&=343\bmod55=13\text{.}\\
\text{Decryption: }\quad m&=c^d\bmod n=13^{27}\bmod55=7\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “Residue class thirteen represents the ciphertext in the model; exponentiation with the private exponent returns seven.” Euler’s congruence arithmetic explains why this works. **This school-level RSA is essentially completely insecure in practice**: the modulus is tiny, and secure padding / encapsulation concepts, randomness and protection against side-channel attacks are absent. Large RSA keys are not resistant to quantum attacks either. The example serves solely to explain residue classes and public and private keys.

**Cross-check using the Chinese remainder theorem:** Instead of calculating $13^{27}$ as an enormous number, we verify the decryption separately modulo 5 and modulo 11:

$$
\begin{aligned}
13^{27}&\equiv3^{27}\equiv3^3=27\equiv2\pmod{5},\\
13^{27}&\equiv2^{27}\equiv2^7=128\equiv7\pmod{11}\text{.}\\
&\text{The number }7\text{ satisfies both conditions:}\\
7\bmod 5&=2\quad\text{and}\quad 7\bmod 11=7\text{.}\\
&\text{Since }5\text{ and }11\text{ are coprime,}\\
&\text{the solution modulo }55\text{ is unique: }m=7\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here we used $3^4\equiv1\pmod5$ and $2^{10}\equiv1\pmod{11}$. The check explains the calculation, **not** the security of the school-level method.

## Why quantum computers matter {#S6}

A powerful error-corrected quantum computer could use **Shor’s algorithm** to solve factorisation and discrete logarithms more efficiently than known classical methods. This threatens, among other things, typical RSA and elliptic-curve protocols. The existence of a sufficiently powerful quantum computer of this kind is not a prerequisite for starting a long-term migration today: intercepted confidential data could potentially be stored and decrypted later (“harvest now, decrypt later”).

For many symmetric primitives, the situation is different: **Grover’s algorithm** changes the theoretical search for keys, but does not simply break all symmetric cryptography. Parameter selection, the security model and the protocol remain important. **Post-quantum** here means algorithms running on ordinary computers that are designed to resist currently known quantum attacks – not necessarily “quantum communication”.

## Lattices and Learning with Errors – what the formula means {#S7}

A mathematical **lattice** consists of integer combinations of given basis vectors. Many lattice problems ask for very short vectors or good approximations. In **Learning with Errors (LWE)**, linear relationships are deliberately perturbed by small errors.

In the simplest one-dimensional *teaching model*, we choose the secret element $s=5$, the modulus $q=17$ and two known coefficients $a_{1}=3$, $a_{2}=7$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_i&=(a_i\cdot s+\eta_i)\bmod17\text{.}\\
\text{First sample: }\quad a_1&=3,\quad \eta_1=1\\
b_1&=(3\cdot5+1)\bmod17=16\text{.}\\
\text{Second sample: }\quad a_2&=7,\quad \eta_2=-1\\
b_2&=(7\cdot5-1)\bmod17=34\bmod17=0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Read aloud: “We multiply a public coefficient by the secret value, add a small error and take the remainder modulo seventeen.” Without errors, these would be ordinary linear equations in a finite residue class structure. With suitably distributed errors, sufficiently large dimension and carefully specified parameters, a different problem arises that is difficult for attackers.

**Limitation of the example:** The two tiny scalar equations can easily be solved by trial and error and do **not constitute a secure LWE system**. Modern **Module-LWE** methods operate on much larger structured objects with specific error distributions, encapsulation / decapsulation mechanisms and protection against tampering or side-channel attacks. No security guarantee may be deduced from the illustrative example.

**Cross-check – two samples do not determine the secret uniquely here:** Let us explicitly assume that the errors may be drawn from $\{-1,0,1\}$. In addition to the original solution $s=5$, $\eta _{1}=1$, $\eta _{2}=-1$, the values $s=0$, $\eta _{1}=-1$, $\eta _{2}=0$ also fit **exactly the same public observations**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{With }s=5:\quad &(3\cdot5+1)\bmod17=16;\\
&(7\cdot5-1)\bmod17=0\text{.}\\
\text{With }s=0:\quad &(3\cdot0-1)\bmod17=16;\\
&(7\cdot0+0)\bmod17=0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Under this error assumption, the two equations therefore admit at least two different secrets. Conversely, the tiny search space would be easy to attack by exhaustive trial. **Ambiguity in a miniature calculation is not a security proof for LWE**; its actual hardness depends on dimension, error distribution and parameters.

## Current post-quantum standards: three different tasks {#S8}

The US standards institute **NIST** published three central final PQC standards in August 2024. The decisive technical difference between them is their **purpose**:

| Standard | Algorithm | Purpose | Underlying mathematical idea |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **FIPS 203** | **ML-KEM** | Establish a shared secret by key encapsulation | Module-LWE / lattice assumptions |
| **FIPS 204** | **ML-DSA** | Create and verify digital signatures | Module-lattice methods |
| **FIPS 205** | **SLH-DSA** | Create and verify digital signatures | Stateless hash-based construction |

Read aloud: “ML-KEM is intended for establishing a secret key; ML-DSA and SLH-DSA are intended for signatures, that is, for verifying origin and integrity.” A KEM does not automatically protect against an active attacker impersonating the other party. Authentication and a correctly designed protocol context are required. Likewise, a digital signature is not a substitute for encrypting confidential data.

The NIST project develops and evaluates further methods. **The current state and recommendations may change**; the following official links are therefore documented with their access date. However, the three FIPS listed above are published standards, not mere research proposals. **Documentation status (4 November 2026):** Notices concerning possible editorial or technical errata have been published on the official NIST pages for FIPS 203 and FIPS 204. For actual implementations, the **current version of the relevant FIPS, its errata and implementation profiles** must therefore be checked; the existence of a standard alone does not imply an error-free library.

## From mathematical hardness to genuinely secure software {#S9}

Cryptographic security is a **conditional** mathematical statement: it depends on a hardness assumption, the adversary model, correct implementations and the particular composition of the protocol. Typical security objectives are defined as games: an attacker attempts to distinguish a message, forge a signature or determine a secret key. “Secure” then means, for example, that the probability of success is sufficiently small given the specified resources, provided the assumptions hold.

When migrating older systems, existing cryptographic dependencies must be identified, new methods integrated into **standardised and tested libraries**, keys and certificates managed, and device and protocol limitations taken into account. A hybrid method combines classical and post-quantum-oriented components to mitigate certain migration risks. Whether and how such a combination provides security depends on the **correct design of the combination**; merely concatenating two secrets proves nothing.

Even large mathematical keys do not protect against compromised endpoints, stolen keys, misconfigurations or side channels. Software checks from M4-L4 and reliable system architecture therefore remain important.

## What is it useful for? {#S10}

Cryptography protects communication channels, software updates, firmware signatures, digital identities, cloud storage and networks of autonomous devices. Post-quantum methods are particularly relevant for long-lived technical systems, because today's devices may need to communicate securely for many years. A basic mathematical understanding helps us avoid treating **key exchange**, **signatures**, **hash functions** and **encryption** as interchangeable terms.

## Assumptions and limitations {#S11}

Our numerical examples are **not implementation templates**. The statements about LWE describe the principle, not the full security proofs of the standardised constructions. Quantum resistance is a security assessment based on the current state of known attacks, not a proof of the impossibility of all future attacks. Even a correctly understood standard can be undermined by poor key storage, random number sources, protocol errors or implementations.
